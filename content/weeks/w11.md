## D1 · Anatomy of the modern decoder block (the "Llama recipe" and after)

The 2023 Llama recipe became the default open-model block; most 2024–2026 models are variations of it:

| Component | 2017 Transformer | Modern default |
|---|---|---|
| Norm | post-LayerNorm | **pre-RMSNorm** (+ final norm) |
| Position | sinusoidal, added | **RoPE** on q, k (large base; partial in some) |
| Attention | MHA | **GQA** (or MLA / hybrids) |
| FFN | ReLU, 4d | **SwiGLU**, ~8d/3 (dense) or **MoE** |
| Biases | everywhere | **none** in linear layers (some keep QKV bias, e.g. Qwen2) |
| Dropout | 0.1 | **0** for pre-training |
| Extras | – | QK-norm, gated attention, logit caps, z-loss, MTP heads |

```math
h = x + \mathrm{Attn}(\mathrm{RMSNorm}(x)),\qquad y = h + \mathrm{FFN}(\mathrm{RMSNorm}(h))
```

**FLOPs per token** ≈ $2N$ for a forward pass ($N$ = non-embedding params *active* per token), plus attention
$\approx 2\cdot2\,L\,T\,d$ for context length $T$; training ≈ $6N$ per token (forward + 2× backward). These
three numbers let you estimate training cost and inference speed in an interview.

**Why each change:** pre-norm for stability at depth; RMSNorm cheaper with equal quality; RoPE for relative
positions and extension; GQA to shrink the KV cache; SwiGLU for better quality per FLOP; removing biases simplifies
and slightly improves stability and quantisation; no dropout because data ≫ parameters.

> every difference. The Transformer hub page in this app has the diagram.

## D2 · From MHA to MQA, GQA and MLA: shrinking the KV cache

During decoding every layer stores keys and values for all previous tokens. Per token:

```math
\text{KV bytes/token}=2\times L\times n_{kv}\times d_h\times \text{bytes}
```

For a Llama-2-7B-like model with MHA ($L=32$, $n_{kv}=32$, $d_h=128$, BF16) that's 512 KB per token — 2 GB for a 4K
context, per sequence. Decoding is memory-bandwidth bound, so the KV cache sets both memory capacity and speed.

* **MQA** (Shazeer 2019): all query heads share **one** K/V head → $n_{kv}=1$. Huge saving, some quality loss and
  training instability; awkward for tensor parallelism.
* **GQA** (Ainslie 2023): $G$ groups of query heads share a K/V head (e.g. 32 Q heads, 8 KV heads → 4× smaller cache).
  Near-MHA quality; the default in Llama 2-70B/3, Qwen, Mistral, Gemma. Existing MHA checkpoints can be **uptrained** to
  GQA by mean-pooling K/V heads within each group and training ~5% more.
* **MLA — Multi-head Latent Attention** (DeepSeek-V2): compress K and V jointly into a low-rank latent
  $c^{KV}_t = W^{DKV}h_t$ (e.g. 512 dims) and cache only that (plus a small shared RoPE key, 64 dims). Keys/values for
  each head are up-projections $k = W^{UK}c$, $v = W^{UV}c$. At inference $W^{UK}$ can be **absorbed** into the query
  projection and $W^{UV}$ into the output projection, so attention runs directly on the latent. Cache per token per
  layer: $512+64=576$ values vs $2\times128\times128$ for 128-head MHA — **~57× smaller**, with quality at or above MHA.
  RoPE is "decoupled" (a separate small rotary part) because rotating the latent would break absorption.
* **Adoption:** DeepSeek V2/V3/R1/V3.2/V4, Kimi K2 (MLA), GLM-5 (MLA + DSA), Kimi K3 (**gated MLA** layers alongside KDA).
  TransMLA and similar methods convert GQA models into MLA.

**Trade-offs:** MQA/GQA reduce cache by sharing; MLA by low-rank compression with more compute per token (good on
GPUs where decode is memory-bound, so extra FLOPs are nearly free). On edge NPUs, the extra matmuls and odd shapes of
MLA can be less friendly than plain GQA — always check the target.

> **Interview angle.** Compute the KV cache for a given config on the spot, and explain why MLA's absorption trick

## D3 · Stability and expressivity tricks

As models and learning rates grew, specific instabilities appeared and got specific fixes:

* **Attention logit growth** → softmax saturates, loss spikes. Fixes: **QK-norm** (RMSNorm on $q$ and $k$ per head:
  Qwen3, OLMo 2, Gemma 3), **logit soft-capping** $c\cdot\tanh(z/c)$ (Gemma 2), **QK-clip** (Kimi K2's MuonClip:
  rescale $W_q,W_k$ when the max logit exceeds a threshold).
* **Output logit drift** → **z-loss** $10^{-4}\log^2 Z$ (PaLM) keeps the softmax normaliser near 1; also helps with
  low-precision.
* **Attention sinks and massive activations.** Many heads dump attention onto the first token (it acts as a
  "no-op" target because softmax must sum to 1), and a few hidden dimensions grow huge — bad for long context and for
  quantisation (outliers!). **StreamingLLM** keeps sink tokens in the cache; **gpt-oss** adds a learned per-head sink
  logit; **gated attention** (Qwen3-Next/Qwen3.5: an input-dependent sigmoid gate on the SDPA output,
  $o = \mathrm{SDPA}(\cdot)\odot\sigma(xW_g)$) lets a head output ~0 without a sink, removing sinks and massive
  activations and improving stability.
* **Hyper-parameter transfer:** **μP** (maximal update parameterisation) scales init and per-layer LRs with width
  so the optimal LR found on a small proxy model transfers to the large one.
* **Sandwich/peri-norms** (extra norm after the sub-layer) in some models (Gemma 2/3, OLMo 2 reorders norms).
* **MTP auxiliary heads** (DeepSeek-V3, Qwen3.5, MiniMax M2): predict 1–2 extra future tokens in training — denser
  signal, and the heads double as a speculative-decoding draft (week 14).

> **Interview angle.** "Why do LLMs have huge activation outliers, and why should an embedded engineer care?" —
> attention-sink behaviour and a few channels with massive values; they wreck per-tensor INT8 quantisation, which is
> why SmoothQuant/rotations exist (week 23) and why gated attention is quantisation-friendly.

## D4 · Rethinking the residual stream: hyper-connections, mHC and Attention Residuals

Pre-norm residual streams add every sub-layer output with weight 1, so the hidden state's norm grows with depth and
each layer's contribution gets **diluted** ("PreNorm dilution"); deeper layers contribute less. Three lines of work
redesign the residual path itself:

**Hyper-connections (HC, ByteDance 2024):** widen the residual stream into $n$ parallel streams and learn how each
layer reads from them ($\mathcal H^{pre}$), writes to them ($\mathcal H^{post}$) and mixes them ($\mathcal H^{res}$):

```math
x_{l+1}=\mathcal H^{res}_l\,x_l+\mathcal H^{post\top}_l\,F\!\big(\mathcal H^{pre}_l x_l\big)
```

The sub-layer still runs at normal width, so FLOPs barely change. Unconstrained $\mathcal H^{res}$, multiplied across
many layers, can explode or vanish — it loses the identity-mapping property.

**mHC — Manifold-Constrained Hyper-Connections (DeepSeek, Dec 2025):** project $\mathcal H^{res}$ onto the **Birkhoff
polytope** of **doubly stochastic** matrices (non-negative, rows and columns sum to 1) using Sinkhorn–Knopp
iterations (alternately normalise rows and columns). Products of doubly stochastic matrices stay doubly stochastic,
so the mixing is norm-preserving "in aggregate" and identity-like stability returns; $\mathcal H^{pre},\mathcal H^{post}$
are kept non-negative. Reported overhead ≈ 6.7% training time at $n=4$ with fused kernels. **DeepSeek-V4** uses mHC
with 4 streams in production. Follow-ups: mHC-lite (exact doubly stochastic via convex combinations of permutation
matrices), sHC (spectral-sphere constraint allowing negative entries).

**Attention Residuals (AttnRes, Moonshot/Kimi, Mar 2026):** treat depth like a sequence. Each layer gets a learned
pseudo-query and takes a **softmax-weighted combination of all previous layer outputs** instead of their plain sum,
so it can selectively retrieve earlier representations. **Block AttnRes** groups layers into ~8 blocks (plain sums
inside a block, attention across block summaries) for <2% inference overhead. Reported: consistent loss gains on
Kimi Linear (48B-A3B); **Kimi K3** is built on KDA + AttnRes.

The common thread: residual connections were "solved" in 2015; frontier labs in 2025–26 found real gains by making
the residual stream wider and/or adaptive, with constraints to keep it stable.

> **Interview angle.** Explain why a doubly stochastic mixing matrix is safe to compose over 60+ layers (its spectral
> norm is ≤ 1 and the all-ones vector is preserved), while an unconstrained one isn't.

## D5 · The evolution timeline and where architectures are heading

* **2017** Transformer. **2018** GPT-1, BERT. **2019** GPT-2 (pre-norm), T5 (relative bias), MQA. **2020** GPT-3 (175B
  dense), scaling laws; Switch/GShard MoE.
* **2021** RoPE, ALiBi. **2022** Chinchilla (compute-optimal data), PaLM (SwiGLU, parallel blocks, z-loss),
  FlashAttention.
* **2023** Llama 1/2 (the open recipe; GQA in 2-70B), Mistral 7B (sliding-window attention), Mixtral 8×7B (open MoE),
  Mamba.
* **2024** DeepSeek-V2 (MLA + fine-grained MoE), Llama 3 (128K vocab, 15T tokens), Gemma 2 (local/global, soft-cap),
  DeepSeek-V3 (671B-A37B, aux-loss-free MoE, MTP, FP8 training). Qwen2.5.
* **2025** DeepSeek-R1 (RL-trained reasoning, GRPO), MiniMax-01/M1 (lightning attention hybrid), Kimi K2 (1T, MuonClip),
  Qwen3 (hybrid thinking, QK-norm), gpt-oss (sinks, alternating SWA), Qwen3-Next (Gated DeltaNet + gated attention),
  DeepSeek-V3.2 (DSA sparse attention), Kimi Linear (KDA), MiniMax M2 (back to full attention), mHC paper.
* **2026** Kimi K2.5 (native multimodal, agent swarm), GLM-5 (MLA + DSA), Qwen3.5 (3:1 Gated DeltaNet hybrid, native
  multimodal), Attention Residuals, DeepSeek-V4 (CSA/HCA hybrid attention, mHC, Muon, FP4 experts, 1M context),
  Kimi K3 (2.8T, KDA + AttnRes, 16/896 experts).

**The pattern.** Compute moved from dense to **sparse** (MoE: activate 3–5% of parameters). The bottleneck moved from
FLOPs to **memory and bandwidth**: KV cache (GQA → MLA → compressed/sparse/linear attention) and weights (FP8 → FP4
QAT). Training efficiency improved through optimisers (Muon), precision (FP8/FP4) and RL infrastructure. Long context
(1M) and agentic workloads drive the attention redesigns. Not everything wins: MiniMax went hybrid with M1 then back
to full attention in M2 because efficient attention underperformed on complex multi-hop tasks that benchmarks missed.

> **Interview angle.** "What's the most important architectural change since the original Transformer?" — a good
> answer picks one (e.g. MoE or KV-cache reduction), explains the bottleneck it addressed, and names the trade-off.

## Questions

??* d1 [architecture] | List the differences between the original Transformer block and a modern LLM block, with the reason for each.
Post-LayerNorm → pre-RMSNorm (stable deep training; cheaper norm). Sinusoidal absolute → RoPE on q,k (relative
positions, cache-friendly, extendable). MHA → GQA/MLA (smaller KV cache for memory-bound decoding). ReLU FFN → SwiGLU
(better quality per FLOP), often replaced by MoE (more parameters at the same compute). Biases removed (simplicity,
stability). No dropout (data ≫ params). Plus QK-norm/soft-capping/z-loss for stability, gated attention to remove
sinks, and MTP heads for denser training signal and speculative decoding.

??* d1 [estimation] | Approximately how many FLOPs does a forward pass cost per token, and training per token?
Forward ≈ 2N FLOPs per token, where N is the number of (active) non-embedding parameters — one multiply and one add per
weight — plus attention ≈ 4·L·T·d for context T. Training ≈ 6N per token (backward is ≈2× forward). So training a 7B
dense model on 2T tokens ≈ 6·7e9·2e12 ≈ 8.4e22 FLOPs; an MoE uses active parameters (e.g. 37B for DeepSeek-V3).

??* d2 [attention, kv-cache] | Compare MHA, MQA, GQA and MLA in terms of KV cache and quality.
KV bytes/token = 2·L·n_kv·d_h·bytes. MHA: n_kv = n_heads (largest, best baseline quality). MQA: n_kv = 1 (smallest, some
quality loss, can be unstable). GQA: n_kv = groups, e.g. 8 (4–8× smaller than MHA, near-MHA quality — the common
default). MLA: caches a low-rank latent (e.g. 512) plus a 64-dim RoPE key per layer regardless of head count — tens of
times smaller than MHA at equal or better quality, at the cost of extra up-projection compute (absorbed at inference)
and more complex kernels.

??* d2 [attention, chinese-models] | How does DeepSeek's MLA work, and what is the absorption trick?
Each token's hidden state is down-projected to a compressed latent c = W_DKV·h (dimension ≈ 512) that is cached; per-head
keys and values are up-projections k = W_UK·c, v = W_UV·c. Because q·k = q·(W_UK c) = (W_UKᵀ q)·c, W_UK can be folded into
the query projection, and W_UV into the output projection, so at inference attention is computed directly against the
latent without materialising per-head K/V. RoPE can't be applied to c without breaking this, so a small separate rotary
key (≈64 dims) shared across heads is added ("decoupled RoPE").

?? d2 [attention] | How can you convert a trained MHA model to GQA?
Group the KV heads (e.g. 32 → 8 groups of 4), initialise each group's K and V projection as the mean of the original heads
in that group, keep the query heads, then continue pre-training ("uptraining") for a small fraction (~5%) of the original
compute to recover quality. This is what the GQA paper did with T5.

??* d3 [stability] | What is an attention sink, and how do gated attention and learned sinks address it?
Softmax forces each head's weights to sum to 1, so a head that has nothing useful to attend to dumps its mass on a
low-information token — usually the first one — which becomes a "sink" and develops massive activations. This hurts
streaming/long context (evicting the sink breaks the model) and creates outliers that harm quantisation. StreamingLLM
keeps sink tokens in the cache; gpt-oss adds a learnable per-head sink logit to the softmax denominator; Qwen3-Next/3.5's
gated attention multiplies the attention output by a sigmoid gate computed from the input, so a head can output ~0
directly, removing the sink and the massive activations.

?? d3 [stability] | What do QK-norm and z-loss each fix?
QK-norm applies RMSNorm to queries and keys per head, bounding attention logits so softmax doesn't saturate when weights
grow — it removes a common source of loss spikes and allows higher learning rates. z-loss adds a small penalty on
log²(Σ exp(logits)) of the output softmax, keeping its normaliser near 1 so output logits don't drift, which improves
stability and low-precision behaviour.

??* d4 [residual, chinese-models] | What problem do hyper-connections solve, and why did DeepSeek constrain them (mHC)?
Standard residuals add each layer with a fixed weight, so in pre-norm models the stream norm grows and individual layers
get diluted. Hyper-connections widen the residual into n streams with learnable read, write and mixing matrices,
improving performance at almost no FLOP cost. But the mixing matrices are multiplied across all layers; unconstrained,
their product can blow up or vanish and the identity-mapping property is lost, destabilising large-scale training. mHC
projects the mixing matrix onto doubly stochastic matrices with Sinkhorn–Knopp iterations; such matrices are closed
under multiplication and have spectral norm ≤ 1, restoring stability. DeepSeek-V4 uses mHC with 4 streams.

?? d4 [residual, chinese-models] | What are Kimi's Attention Residuals?
Instead of summing all previous layer outputs with weight 1, each layer computes a softmax attention over the outputs of
earlier layers (depth acts like a sequence), using a learned per-layer pseudo-query, so it can choose which earlier
representations to use. Block AttnRes keeps plain residuals within blocks of layers and attends over block summaries,
cutting overhead to about 2% at inference. It gives consistent loss improvements and is part of Kimi K3's architecture.

??* d5 [evolution] | Summarise how transformer architectures evolved to be more efficient from 2017 to 2026.
Compute: dense FFNs → sparse MoE with fine-grained and shared experts (3–5% of parameters active). Memory: MHA → GQA → MLA,
then sliding-window, linear (Gated DeltaNet, KDA, Lightning) and learned sparse/compressed attention (DSA, CSA/HCA) to
shrink KV cache and long-context cost. Precision: FP32 → BF16 → FP8 training → FP4 QAT. Optimisation: AdamW → Muon.
Stability: pre-norm, RMSNorm, QK-norm, gated attention, constrained residual mixing (mHC, AttnRes). Decoding: MTP heads
for speculative decoding. Each change targeted the bottleneck of its time: FLOPs, then memory bandwidth and context length.

?? d5 [evolution, chinese-models] | Why did MiniMax move from hybrid linear attention (M1) back to full attention (M2)?
Their small-scale hybrid models matched full attention on standard leaderboards, but at scale they found weaknesses on
complex, multi-hop reasoning and agentic tasks that those benchmarks didn't capture, and the efficient-attention
infrastructure (kernels, caching, precision) was less mature. They concluded that, for an industrial system, efficient
attention wasn't yet clearly better than full attention, and that the real bottleneck was evaluation. It's a good example
that architecture choices must be validated on the target workload, not proxy benchmarks.
