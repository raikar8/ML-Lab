## D1 · Why LLMs are hard to quantise

* **Outlier features:** beyond ~6.7B parameters, a few hidden dimensions carry activations 20–100× larger than the rest, systematically
  across tokens (LLM.int8(), Dettmers 2022). Per-tensor INT8 activations then lose almost all resolution for normal channels.
* **Massive activations / attention sinks** (week 11): a handful of extreme values on specific tokens.
* Weights are comparatively well-behaved (near-Gaussian, few outliers) → **weight-only** quantisation is easy at 8 bits and workable at
  4 bits; **activation** quantisation is the hard part.

**Taxonomy of schemes:**

| Scheme | Weights | Activations | Where it wins |
|---|---|---|---|
| W8A16 / W4A16 | int8/int4 (group-wise) | BF16/FP16 | decode (memory-bound): dequantise weights in the kernel |
| W8A8 | int8/FP8 | int8/FP8 | prefill/large batch (compute-bound): integer/FP8 Tensor Cores |
| W4A8 / 8da4w | int4 | int8 dynamic per-token | edge CPUs/NPUs (ExecuTorch "8da4w") |
| KV quant | – | KV cache FP8/int4 | long context, large batch |

**LLM.int8():** decompose the matmul — outlier columns in FP16, the rest in int8 (vector-wise scales). Accurate but slow; it motivated
the methods below.

> **Interview angle.** "Why is weight-only 4-bit quantisation enough to speed up decoding?" — decode is memory-bound; halving or
> quartering weight bytes speeds it up proportionally even though the math still runs in FP16 after in-register dequantisation.

## D2 · Weight-only PTQ: GPTQ, AWQ and llama.cpp k-quants

**GPTQ** (Frantar et al. 2022) — layer-wise, second-order. Minimise the layer's output error on calibration inputs $X$:
$\|WX-\hat WX\|^2$. The Hessian is $H=2XX^\top$. Quantise weights **one column (input dimension) at a time**; after quantising column
$j$, distribute its error onto the not-yet-quantised columns using the inverse Hessian (the Optimal Brain Surgeon update):

```math
\delta=\frac{w_j-\mathrm{quant}(w_j)}{[H^{-1}]_{jj}},\qquad W_{:,j+1:}\ \leftarrow\ W_{:,j+1:}-\delta\,[H^{-1}]_{j,j+1:}
```

Done efficiently with a Cholesky factor of $H^{-1}$ and lazy block updates; a 175B model quantises in a few GPU-hours. Dampening
($H+\lambda I$) and "act-order" (quantise columns with large $H_{jj}$ first) improve results. Typical config: 4-bit, group size 128.

**AWQ** (Lin et al. 2023): ~1% of weight channels are **salient** — those multiplying large activations. Protect them not by keeping
them in FP16 but by **scaling**: multiply input channel $j$ of $W$ by $s_j$ and divide the activation by $s_j$ (fold into the previous
op). Larger $s_j$ reduces relative rounding error for that channel. Search $s=\bar{|x|}^\alpha$ over $\alpha\in[0,1]$ per layer to
minimise output error. No backprop, fast, robust; popular for on-device (MLC, TinyChat).

**llama.cpp / GGUF k-quants:** super-blocks of 256 weights split into sub-blocks (e.g. 8 × 32) with quantised sub-block scales/mins, so
Q4_K ≈ 4.5 bits/weight; variants Q2_K–Q6_K, the `_S/_M/_L` suffixes mix precisions per tensor type (e.g. keep attention V and FFN down
projections higher). An **importance matrix** (imatrix, from calibration activations) weights the rounding error like AWQ/GPTQ. Q8_0 is
near-lossless; Q4_K_M is the common default.

**Other:** HQQ (calibration-free, fast), EXL2/EXL3 (mixed bit-rates), AQLM / QuIP# (vector quantisation, 2-bit).

> **Interview angle.** Explain in one breath why GPTQ is better than RTN: it compensates each column's quantisation error by adjusting
> the remaining weights, using second-order (Hessian) information from real activations.

## D3 · Activation quantisation: SmoothQuant and rotations

**SmoothQuant** (Xiao et al. 2022) migrates the difficulty from activations to weights with a per-input-channel scale $s_j$:

```math
Y=(X\,\mathrm{diag}(s)^{-1})\cdot(\mathrm{diag}(s)\,W),\qquad s_j=\frac{\max|X_{:,j}|^{\alpha}}{\max|W_{j,:}|^{1-\alpha}},\ \alpha\approx0.5
```

Activation outlier channels are shrunk, weights absorb the scale (weights quantise easily). The $X$-side scaling is folded into the
preceding LayerNorm/linear, so it's free at inference. Enables **W8A8** for OPT/BLOOM/Llama with minimal loss.

**Rotations / incoherence processing** (QuIP, QuaRot, SpinQuant): multiply by an orthogonal matrix $Q$: $Y=(XQ)(Q^\top W)$. A
random **Hadamard** rotation spreads outlier energy across all channels (makes distributions near-Gaussian, low kurtosis), so both
activations and weights quantise well — enabling **W4A4** and 4-bit KV cache. Hadamard transforms are fast ($O(d\log d)$, like an FFT
without multiplies) and can be fused/absorbed into adjacent weights for most layers; **SpinQuant** learns the rotations. The function
is unchanged because $QQ^\top=I$ — the same "equivalent transformation" idea as CLE and SmoothQuant.

**KV-cache quantisation:** **KIVI** — keys per-channel (outlier channels are consistent across tokens), values per-token, with a small
full-precision window of recent tokens; 2-bit KV with small loss. FP8 KV is the safe default on GPUs.

**On-device default (ExecuTorch / LiteRT / llama.cpp CPU):** group-wise int4 weights (group 32–128) + int8 dynamic per-token
activations ("8da4w"), 8-bit embeddings, often FP16/int8 KV.

> **Interview angle.** Show the "equivalent transformation" family: CLE (rescale between layers), SmoothQuant (rescale between activation
> and weight), rotations (orthogonal transform) — all keep the float function identical while making quantisation easier.

## D4 · Floating-point and micro-scaled formats

**FP8:** E4M3 (more precision, max 448) for weights/activations, E5M2 (more range) for gradients. Scales are still needed (per-tensor or
finer — DeepSeek-V3 used 1×128 / 128×128 blocks). Non-uniform level spacing suits bell-shaped data better than INT8 at the same bits in
many cases.

**Micro-scaling (MX) formats (OCP spec, 2023):** blocks of **32** elements share one **E8M0** scale (a pure power of two), elements in
FP8 (MXFP8), FP6, **FP4 E2M1** (MXFP4) or INT8. Block scaling confines outliers to their block — hardware-native fine-grained
quantisation (it answers DeepSeek's ISCA "hardware lessons" request). gpt-oss shipped MoE weights in MXFP4; **Kimi K3** ships MXFP4 weights
/ MXFP8 activations with QAT.

**NVFP4** (NVIDIA Blackwell): FP4 E2M1 elements, blocks of **16** with an **E4M3** scale (non-power-of-two, more accurate) plus a
per-tensor FP32 scale. Better accuracy than MXFP4 at the same bit width; Blackwell Tensor Cores run FP4 natively at ~2× FP8 throughput.

**FP4 values (E2M1):** {0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6} and negatives — only 15 distinct values, so the block scale does most of the work.

**FP4/INT4 QAT at frontier scale:** DeepSeek-V4 trains MoE expert weights with FP4 QAT; Kimi K2 Thinking ships INT4 weights from QAT;
Apple's on-device foundation models use very low-bit (2-bit-class) QAT for their ~3B model. The lesson for embedded: when you control
training, QAT to 4 bits is now standard practice, not research.

**INT vs FP at 4 bits:** INT4 has uniform levels (good for uniform-ish data after rotation); FP4 has finer levels near zero (good for
bell-shaped data); with small blocks and good scales they're close — hardware support usually decides.

> **Interview angle.** "What is MXFP4?" — 4-bit E2M1 floats in blocks of 32 sharing an 8-bit power-of-two scale: ≈4.25 bits/value,
> outliers contained per block, natively supported by new accelerators.

## D5 · Extreme low-bit, evaluation, and choosing a scheme

**BitNet b1.58** (Microsoft): weights **ternary** {−1, 0, +1} via absmean scaling ($\tilde W=\mathrm{round}(W/\overline{|W|})$ clipped
to [−1,1]), activations int8; **trained from scratch** with QAT (STE). Matmuls become additions/subtractions — attractive for CPUs and
custom silicon (bitnet.cpp). Post-training ternarisation of an ordinary model doesn't work; it must be trained that way.

**2–3-bit PTQ:** vector quantisation (AQLM, QuIP# with lattice codebooks + Hadamard incoherence) beats scalar quantisation at 2 bits;
quality drops noticeably for small models.

**Binary/ternary nets on MCUs:** XNOR + popcount; used for tiny KWS/vision with QAT.

**Evaluating quantised LLMs:** perplexity is necessary but not sufficient — check task accuracy (reasoning/maths and long-context
degrade first), **KL divergence** of output distributions vs the float model, "flip rate" of answers, long generations (errors compound
in reasoning chains), and multilingual/code subsets. Small models are more fragile than large ones; over-trained models (many tokens/param)
are harder to quantise.

**Choosing a scheme for a device (decision recipe):**

1. What does the hardware run natively? (int8 only → W8A8 static; int4 weights + int8 acts → 8da4w; FP8/FP4 on new GPUs/NPUs.)
2. Is the workload decode-bound (weight-only low-bit pays) or prefill/compute-bound (needs low-bit activations)?
3. Memory budget → bits per weight (and KV).
4. Start PTQ (RTN → AWQ/GPTQ, SmoothQuant/rotation for activations), measure; if insufficient, QAT or distillation-aware QAT.
5. Validate on device: bit-exact kernels, latency, memory, quality on the real task.

> **Interview angle.** Given "3B model, phone NPU supports int8 and int4 weights, 8 GB RAM, chat assistant" — propose W4 (group 32–64,
> AWQ/GPTQ or QAT) with int8 dynamic activations, int8/FP16 KV with a sliding window, 8-bit embeddings; estimate memory and tokens/s.

## Questions

??* d1 [quantization, llm] | Why are LLM activations harder to quantise than weights?
Weights are roughly Gaussian with few outliers, so group-wise scales quantise them well even at 4 bits. Activations in large LLMs contain
systematic outlier channels (a few hidden dimensions 20–100× larger than the rest, consistently across tokens) and massive activations tied
to attention sinks. A per-tensor or per-token scale must cover the outliers, leaving the normal channels with very few levels; per-channel
activation scales would fix it but can't be used in an efficient integer matmul (the scale would vary along the reduction dimension).

??* d2 [gptq] | How does GPTQ work?
For each linear layer, collect calibration inputs X and form the Hessian H = 2XXᵀ of the reconstruction loss ||WX − ŴX||². Quantise the weight
matrix one input column at a time; after quantising column j, compute its error divided by [H⁻¹]_jj and subtract that error times the
corresponding row of H⁻¹ from the remaining unquantised columns, so they compensate for it (Optimal Brain Surgeon update). Using a Cholesky
factorisation of H⁻¹ and blocked lazy updates makes it fast; dampening and act-order improve stability and accuracy. Result: accurate 3–4-bit
weight-only quantisation in GPU-hours.

??* d2 [awq] | What is the key idea of AWQ?
A small fraction of weight channels matter most — those multiplied by large-magnitude activations. Instead of keeping them in higher precision
(hardware-unfriendly mixed formats), AWQ scales those input channels of the weights up by s_j (and the activations down by s_j, folded into the
preceding op), which reduces their relative rounding error after quantisation. The per-channel scales s = mean|x|^α are found by a quick grid
search over α per layer minimising output error, using only a small calibration set and no backprop.

?? d2 [llama.cpp] | What do GGUF quant names like Q4_K_M mean?
Q4_K is a "k-quant": weights in super-blocks of 256 split into sub-blocks with their own quantised scales/minimums, about 4.5 bits per weight on
average. The suffix (S/M/L) chooses a mix of precisions per tensor type — M keeps some sensitive tensors (e.g. attention V or FFN down projections)
at higher bit widths. Q8_0 is block-wise 8-bit, near-lossless; Q2_K–Q6_K trade size for quality. An importance matrix from calibration data can
guide rounding.

??* d3 [smoothquant] | Explain SmoothQuant's scaling and why it doesn't change the model's output.
For Y = XW, insert diag(s)⁻¹diag(s): Y = (X diag(s)⁻¹)(diag(s) W). Choose per-input-channel s_j = max|X_j|^α / max|W_j|^{1−α} (α ≈ 0.5) so outlier
activation channels are divided down and the corresponding weight rows scaled up, balancing quantisation difficulty between activations and weights.
The product is mathematically identical; the activation scaling is folded into the previous LayerNorm or linear layer offline, so inference cost is
unchanged while W8A8 becomes accurate.

??* d3 [rotation] | Why do Hadamard rotations help quantisation, and why don't they change the model?
Multiplying activations by an orthogonal (randomised Hadamard) matrix Q and weights by Qᵀ leaves XW unchanged since QQᵀ = I. The rotation spreads
the energy of a few outlier channels evenly across all channels, making distributions near-Gaussian with low kurtosis and small max/RMS ratios,
so both activations and weights quantise with much lower error — enabling W4A4 and 4-bit KV caches (QuaRot, SpinQuant). Hadamard transforms are
cheap (O(d log d)) and most can be absorbed into adjacent weight matrices offline.

??* d4 [formats] | Compare FP8 E4M3, MXFP4 and NVFP4.
FP8 E4M3: 8-bit float, 4 exponent/3 mantissa bits, max 448, used with per-tensor or block scales for weights/activations (E5M2 for gradients).
MXFP4: 4-bit E2M1 elements in blocks of 32 sharing an E8M0 power-of-two scale (~4.25 bits/value), an OCP standard. NVFP4: E2M1 elements in blocks of
16 with an FP8 E4M3 scale plus a per-tensor FP32 scale (~4.5 bits/value) — smaller blocks and more precise scales give better accuracy than MXFP4.
Both FP4 formats are natively accelerated on Blackwell-class hardware; MXFP4/MXFP8 are used in released models like gpt-oss and Kimi K3.

?? d4 [formats] | What values can FP4 E2M1 represent, and why do micro-scaled formats need small blocks?
With 1 sign, 2 exponent and 1 mantissa bit: ±{0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6}. With so few levels, quality depends almost entirely on the block scale
matching the local range. Small blocks (16–32 elements) mean an outlier only degrades its own block, and the scale tracks local variation, keeping
error manageable; per-tensor FP4 would be unusable.

??* d5 [low-bit] | What is BitNet b1.58 and can you convert an existing model to it?
A transformer whose linear-layer weights are ternary {−1, 0, +1} (log₂3 ≈ 1.58 bits) using an absmean scale per tensor, with int8 activations,
trained from scratch with quantisation-aware training (STE). Matrix multiplies reduce to additions and subtractions, cutting memory and energy;
bitnet.cpp runs it efficiently on CPUs. Post-training conversion of a normal model to ternary loses too much quality; it has to be trained (or at
least extensively continued-trained) in that form.

?? d5 [evaluation] | How would you evaluate whether a 4-bit quantised LLM is good enough to ship?
Compare against the float model on: perplexity; KL divergence of next-token distributions on representative prompts; task benchmarks that stress
weak points (multi-step maths, code, long-context retrieval, multilingual, tool-call formatting); answer flip rate on your product's prompts;
long-generation stability; and human/LLM-judge preference on real use cases. Also measure latency, memory and energy on the target device with the
actual kernels, since the deployed numerics may differ from simulation.
