## D1 · k-means

**Objective:** partition points into $K$ clusters minimising within-cluster squared distance (inertia)
$J=\sum_i\|x_i-\mu_{c(i)}\|^2$.

**Lloyd's algorithm:** alternate (1) assign each point to the nearest centroid, (2) move each centroid to the
mean of its points. Each step can only decrease $J$, so it converges — to a **local** minimum. Cost per
iteration $O(nKd)$; the assignment is a distance matrix computed with one matrix multiply.

**Initialisation matters.** **k-means++**: pick the first centre at random, then each next centre with
probability proportional to $D(x)^2$ (squared distance to the nearest chosen centre). It is
$O(\log K)$-competitive in expectation and is the default everywhere. Also run several restarts and keep the
lowest inertia.

**Choosing K:** elbow in the inertia curve, silhouette score, or the downstream task (e.g. codebook size).

**Limitations:** assumes roughly spherical, equally sized clusters (it's the hard-assignment, equal-variance
limit of a GMM); sensitive to scaling and outliers; empty clusters must be handled (re-seed them).

**Where you'll see it again:** vector quantisation — codebooks for speech tokenisers and neural codecs (residual
VQ in EnCodec/SoundStream), product quantisation for ANN search, k-means weight sharing for compression
(Deep Compression), and clustering embeddings for speaker diarisation.

> **Interview angle.** Write the vectorised assignment step: `d = (X**2).sum(1)[:,None] - 2*X@C.T +
> (C**2).sum(1)[None]`, then `argmin(1)`. Mention empty clusters and k-means++ without being asked.

## D2 · Gaussian mixtures and the EM algorithm

A GMM models $p(x)=\sum_k\pi_k\,\mathcal N(x\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)$. The latent variable $z$ says which component
generated $x$. Maximising the likelihood directly is awkward (log of a sum), so **EM** alternates:

**E-step** — responsibilities (posterior over $z$):

```math
\gamma_{ik}=\frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_j\pi_j\,\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}
```

**M-step** — weighted MLE with $N_k=\sum_i\gamma_{ik}$:

```math
\pi_k=\frac{N_k}{n},\quad \mu_k=\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}x_i,\quad \Sigma_k=\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k)(x_i-\mu_k)^\top
```

**Why it works:** EM maximises the ELBO $\log p(x)\ge\mathbb E_q[\log p(x,z)]-\mathbb E_q[\log q(z)]$; the
E-step makes the bound tight ($q=p(z\mid x)$), the M-step raises it. The likelihood never decreases, but you get
local optima.

**Practicalities:** compute in log space with log-sum-exp; add a small ridge to covariances ($\Sigma+\epsilon I$)
to avoid singularities when a component collapses onto one point; initialise from k-means; choose K with BIC.

**k-means is a special case:** equal spherical covariances $\sigma^2I$ with $\sigma\to0$ make responsibilities
hard 0/1.

You've already met EM in your SSP work: **Baum–Welch** for HMMs is EM with forward–backward in the E-step. The
ELBO reappears in VAEs (week 16) and diffusion models.

> **Interview angle.** "What's the difference between k-means and GMM?" — hard vs soft assignments, spherical vs
> full covariance, distance vs likelihood, and GMM gives a density you can use for anomaly scores.

## D3 · PCA, SVD and low-rank structure

**PCA** finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance. For centred data $X$ ($n\times d$), the covariance is
$C=\frac{1}{n-1}X^\top X$; its eigenvectors are the principal components and eigenvalues the variances along them.

**Via SVD** (preferred numerically): $X=U\Sigma V^\top$. Columns of $V$ are the components, the variance along
component $j$ is $\sigma_j^2/(n-1)$, and the projected data is $XV_k=U_k\Sigma_k$.

**Eckart–Young theorem:** the best rank-$k$ approximation of a matrix in Frobenius (and spectral) norm is the
truncated SVD:

```math
\min_{\mathrm{rank}(B)\le k}\|A-B\|_F = \|A-U_k\Sigma_kV_k^\top\|_F=\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{j>k}\sigma_j^2}
```

This one theorem underlies a lot of model optimisation: low-rank factorisation of weight matrices
($W\approx AB$, cutting params from $mn$ to $k(m+n)$), **LoRA** (the update $\Delta W$ is constrained to low
rank), MLA in DeepSeek (KV compressed through a low-rank latent), and SVD-based compression of embeddings.

**Choosing k:** cumulative explained variance (e.g. 95%), a scree plot, or downstream accuracy.

**Whitening:** $XV\Sigma^{-1}$ makes the covariance identity — used before ICA and in some feature pipelines.

**Non-linear embeddings for visualisation:** t-SNE preserves local neighbourhoods (cluster sizes and distances
between clusters are not meaningful; sensitive to perplexity); UMAP is faster and keeps somewhat more global
structure. Never use either for quantitative conclusions.

> **Interview angle.** "PCA on unscaled features" — the feature with the largest units dominates. Standardise
> first, unless all features share units (e.g. spectrogram bins).

## D4 · Density estimation and anomaly detection

Anomaly detection matters in industry (sensor faults, bad recordings, fraud) and comes up as a design question.

* **Statistical:** z-scores per feature; **Mahalanobis distance** $\sqrt{(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)}$
  accounts for correlations — for Gaussian data its square is $\chi^2_d$, which gives a principled threshold.
* **Density models:** GMM log-likelihood, kernel density estimation (struggles in high dimension).
* **Isolation forest:** random trees with random splits; anomalies are isolated in fewer splits, so their
  average path length is short. Fast, works in moderate dimension, no distance metric needed.
* **One-class SVM:** learns a boundary around normal data.
* **Reconstruction-based:** train an autoencoder (or PCA) on normal data; anomalies reconstruct poorly. Watch out:
  a powerful autoencoder can reconstruct anomalies too.
* **Embedding-based:** k-NN distance in a pretrained embedding space — often the strongest simple baseline.

**Evaluation is the hard part:** anomalies are rare and diverse, so you usually have few labels. Use
precision@k, PR-AUC on a small labelled set, and alarms per day.

**Out-of-distribution detection** for deployed models is the same problem: max softmax probability is a weak
baseline (overconfident nets), energy scores and Mahalanobis on features do better.

> **Interview angle.** For an in-car audio product: "flag recordings where the microphone is broken or
> clipping" — start with DSP features (RMS, clipping ratio, spectral flatness) and a robust statistical threshold
> before reaching for a neural model.

## D5 · Messy data: imbalance, missing values, label noise and distribution shift

**Class imbalance:** use the right metric (week 2); class weights or **focal loss**; resampling; threshold
tuning; and, often best, collect more minority data or use targeted augmentation.

**Missing data:** MCAR (completely at random), MAR (depends on observed data), MNAR (depends on the missing value
itself — the hardest). Options: drop, impute (mean/median, model-based, iterative), add missingness indicators,
or use models that handle NaN.

**Label noise:** inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's κ) sets your ceiling; find likely errors with
confident learning / high-loss samples; robust losses (symmetric CE, generalised CE); co-teaching.

**Distribution shift** (very relevant for deployed audio):

* **Covariate shift:** $p(x)$ changes, $p(y\mid x)$ doesn't — a new car model with different cabin acoustics.
  Importance weighting $p_{test}(x)/p_{train}(x)$ or domain adaptation.
* **Label/prior shift:** $p(y)$ changes — wake-word frequency differs by market. Re-estimate priors and adjust
  the threshold.
* **Concept drift:** $p(y\mid x)$ changes over time — retrain.

Detect shift by monitoring input statistics, embedding drift (MMD, a classifier trained to tell train from
production data) and prediction-distribution changes.

**Data-centric levers** often beat model changes: better labels, deduplication, targeted collection, synthetic
data (room-impulse-response convolution and noise mixing for speech), curriculum.

> **Interview angle.** "Your model works in the lab but fails in the field" — walk through: shift type
> (devices, environments, users), how to detect it, short-term fix (thresholds, calibration), long-term fix (data
> collection, augmentation, adaptation), and how to monitor.

## Questions

??* d1 [clustering] | Walk me through k-means. Does it always converge, and to what?
Initialise K centroids (k-means++), then repeat: assign each point to its nearest centroid; recompute each
centroid as the mean of its points. Both steps never increase the within-cluster sum of squares, and there are
finitely many assignments, so it converges in finitely many steps — but to a local minimum that depends on the
initialisation. Use k-means++ and multiple restarts; handle empty clusters by re-seeding.

?? d1 [clustering] | How does k-means++ initialisation work, and why is it better?
Choose the first centre uniformly at random; choose each subsequent centre from the data with probability
proportional to its squared distance to the nearest centre already chosen. This spreads the centres out, avoids
putting two centres in one true cluster, gives an O(log K) approximation guarantee in expectation, and typically
converges faster to a better optimum than random initialisation.

??* d2 [em, probability] | Explain the EM algorithm for a Gaussian mixture.
E-step: compute responsibilities γᵢₖ = πₖN(xᵢ|μₖ,Σₖ)/Σⱼ πⱼN(xᵢ|μⱼ,Σⱼ), the posterior probability that point i came
from component k. M-step: re-estimate πₖ = Nₖ/n, μₖ as the γ-weighted mean, Σₖ as the γ-weighted covariance, where
Nₖ = Σᵢγᵢₖ. Each iteration doesn't decrease the log-likelihood because EM performs coordinate ascent on the ELBO.
Compute in log space, regularise covariances, initialise with k-means, pick K with BIC.

?? d2 [em] | Why does EM never decrease the likelihood?
For any distribution q(z), log p(x) = ELBO(q, θ) + KL(q || p(z|x,θ)). The E-step sets q = p(z|x,θ_old), making the
KL zero so the ELBO equals the log-likelihood. The M-step maximises the ELBO over θ with q fixed, which can only
increase it; since KL ≥ 0, the new log-likelihood is at least the new ELBO, which is at least the old
log-likelihood.

??* d3 [pca, linear-algebra] | How are PCA and SVD related? Which would you use to compute PCA?
For centred data X = UΣVᵀ, the covariance XᵀX/(n−1) = VΣ²Vᵀ/(n−1), so the right singular vectors are the
principal directions and σⱼ²/(n−1) are the variances. Compute PCA with SVD of X (or randomised SVD for big
matrices): it avoids forming XᵀX, which squares the condition number and loses precision for small components.

??* d3 [low-rank, compression] | State the Eckart–Young theorem and give two places it's used in modern deep learning.
The best rank-k approximation of A in Frobenius or spectral norm is its truncated SVD, with error
sqrt(Σ_{j>k} σⱼ²) (Frobenius). Uses: low-rank factorisation of weight matrices to compress layers (W ≈ U_kΣ_kV_kᵀ
split into two thin layers); LoRA fine-tuning (constraining ΔW to rank r, initialisable from the SVD as in
PiSSA); DeepSeek's MLA, which stores a low-rank latent instead of full keys and values; and SVD-based analysis of
which layers are compressible.

?? d4 [anomaly] | How does an isolation forest detect anomalies?
It builds trees by picking a random feature and a random threshold between its min and max, recursively, until
points are isolated. Anomalies are few and different, so they get separated near the root: their average path
length across trees is short. The score normalises the path length by the expected path length of an unsuccessful
BST search. It's fast, scale-invariant per feature, and doesn't need a distance metric.

?? d4 [anomaly, ood] | Why is maximum softmax probability a weak out-of-distribution detector, and what's better?
Deep classifiers are overconfident and can assign high softmax probability to inputs far from the training data,
because softmax only compares logits relative to each other. Better: energy score (log-sum-exp of logits),
Mahalanobis distance on penultimate-layer features with class-conditional Gaussians, k-NN distance in feature space,
or training with outlier exposure.

??* d5 [data, deployment] | Explain covariate shift, label shift and concept drift with examples from audio.
Covariate shift: p(x) changes, p(y|x) stays — a new car cabin or microphone changes the acoustics, but the words
spoken map to the same transcripts. Label shift: p(y) changes — the wake word is used more often in one market;
adjust priors and thresholds. Concept drift: p(y|x) itself changes — new slang or a renamed voice command. Detect
with input/embedding drift monitors and prediction-distribution changes; fix with augmentation, adaptation,
recalibration or retraining.

?? d5 [imbalance, losses] | What is focal loss and when would you use it?
FL = −(1−p_t)^γ log p_t, often with a class weight α. The (1−p_t)^γ factor down-weights easy, well-classified
examples so training focuses on hard ones, which helps under extreme imbalance where easy negatives dominate the
loss (it was introduced for dense object detection). γ≈2 is typical. It changes the calibration of the output
probabilities, so recalibrate if you need probabilities.
