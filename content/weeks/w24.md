## D1 · Pruning fundamentals

**Unstructured (weight) pruning:** zero individual weights with the smallest magnitude, globally or per layer; iterate prune → fine-tune
(Han et al. 2015). 80–90% sparsity is often possible in over-parameterised CNNs with small loss. **But** sparse weights only speed up
inference with sparse kernels/hardware; on most CPUs/NPUs dense int8 is faster until ~90%+ sparsity. Storage can still shrink
(compressed formats).

**Lottery ticket hypothesis:** dense networks contain sparse subnetworks that, trained from their original initialisation (or an early
rewind point), match the full network. Insight into over-parameterisation; not a practical speed-up recipe.

**Structured pruning** removes whole channels/filters, attention heads, FFN neurons or layers — producing a smaller *dense* model that
runs faster everywhere. Importance criteria:

* L1/L2 norm of each filter;
* **BN scaling factors** (network slimming: add L1 on BN γ during training, prune channels with small γ);
* **Taylor / gradient-based** importance: estimated loss change $|\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial a}a|$ when removing a unit;
* activation statistics, or learned gates.

Pruning a channel also removes the matching input channel in the next layer (dependency groups; residual connections tie channels across
blocks — tools like Torch-Pruning track these).

**Schedules:** one-shot vs iterative/gradual (cubic sparsity schedule, Zhu & Gupta); prune during training vs after.

> **Interview angle.** "We pruned 70% of weights but latency didn't change" — unstructured sparsity isn't exploited by dense kernels;
> use structured or 2:4 sparsity, or a sparse runtime.

## D2 · Semi-structured sparsity and LLM pruning

**N:M sparsity:** in every group of M consecutive weights, at most N are non-zero. **2:4** is supported by NVIDIA Ampere+ sparse Tensor
Cores (~2× math throughput, ~1.5× in practice) and some NPUs; stored as values + 2-bit indices. Fine-tune after masking to recover accuracy.

**LLM one-shot pruning** (no retraining is affordable at scale):

* **SparseGPT:** OBS-style one-shot pruning with Hessian-based weight updates (same machinery as GPTQ); 50–60% unstructured or 2:4 on
  large models with modest loss.
* **Wanda:** score weights by $|W_{ij}|\cdot\|X_j\|_2$ (magnitude × input activation norm), prune per output row, **no weight update** —
  nearly as good as SparseGPT, far simpler. Activation-awareness matters because of LLM outlier features.
* **Structured LLM pruning + distillation:** LLM-Pruner, Sheared-LLaMA, NVIDIA **Minitron** (prune width — heads, FFN neurons, embedding
  channels — and/or depth, then distil from the original on a small fraction of tokens; Llama-3.1-Minitron 4B from 8B). Depth pruning
  (removing layers with the most similar input/output, ShortGPT) is simple and gives real speed-ups.
* Sparsity and quantisation compose (e.g. 2:4 + int4), but errors add up.

> **Interview angle.** "Width or depth pruning for an on-device LLM?" — depth pruning gives larger latency gains (fewer sequential layers)
> but tends to lose more accuracy; width pruning keeps quality better; both need distillation to recover.

## D3 · Knowledge distillation

**Hinton et al. (2015):** train the student on the teacher's softened distribution:

```math
\mathcal L=\alpha\,\mathrm{CE}(y,\ p_s)+(1-\alpha)\,T^2\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathrm{softmax}(z_t/T)\,\|\,\mathrm{softmax}(z_s/T)\big)
```

Temperature $T>1$ reveals "dark knowledge" (relative probabilities of wrong classes). The $T^2$ factor keeps gradient magnitudes
comparable across temperatures (soft-target gradients scale as $1/T^2$).

**Variants:**

* **Feature/hint distillation** (FitNets): match intermediate features via a projection; attention transfer.
* **Sequence-level KD** (Kim & Rush): train on teacher-generated outputs (beam search) — the basis of LLM SFT distillation
  (R1-Distill).
* **On-policy / generalised KD:** student samples, teacher scores (reverse KL) — avoids exposure bias (week 17).
* **Self-distillation / born-again networks; ensemble teachers.**
* **Data-free KD** using generated or synthetic inputs.
* **For audio:** distil a large enhancement/ASR model into a streaming student (frame-level targets, teacher masks or logits); KWS students
  from large audio encoders.

**Practical issues:** capacity gap (a much larger teacher can be worse — use a teacher assistant), matching tokenizers/vocabularies for LLM
logit distillation, temperature tuning, and label-smoothed teachers carrying less information.

> **Interview angle.** Explain why $T^2$ appears: the gradient of the soft KL w.r.t. student logits is $\frac1T(p_s^T-p_t^T)$ and both
> soft distributions shrink differences by $1/T$, so overall the gradient scales as $1/T^2$; multiplying by $T^2$ keeps the soft and hard
> terms balanced.

## D4 · Efficient architecture design and hardware-aware NAS

**Design guidelines** (ShuffleNetV2, measured on real hardware): equal input/output channel widths minimise memory access; excessive
group convolution increases memory cost; network fragmentation (many small parallel branches) hurts parallelism; element-wise ops are not
free. Prefer fewer, wider, regular layers that the target's kernels like; keep tensor shapes aligned to vector widths (multiples of 8/16/32).

**Hardware-aware NAS:**

* **MnasNet:** RL search with a reward combining accuracy and measured latency on a phone → MobileNetV3-style blocks.
* **FBNet / DARTS-style:** differentiable search with a latency lookup table.
* **Once-for-All (OFA):** train one supernet supporting many depths/widths/kernel sizes/resolutions; extract specialised subnets for each
  device without retraining.
* **MCUNet:** TinyNAS (optimise the search space for MCU memory) + TinyEngine (memory-efficient inference library) — ImageNet on
  microcontrollers.

**Small transformers / LMs for the edge:** MobileViT, EfficientFormer, FastViT for vision; for language, **SmolLM**, **Qwen3.5 0.8B–4B**
(hybrid DeltaNet), Llama 3.2 1B/3B, Phi-4-mini, **Gemma 3n** (MatFormer nested sub-models you can slice to different sizes, and Per-Layer
Embeddings cached off-accelerator to cut on-chip memory). Distillation from large teachers and very long training (many tokens/param) are
what make them good.

**Dynamic/conditional computation:** early exit, cascades (tiny always-on model gates a bigger one — standard for wake words), MoE,
token/frame skipping (skip enhancement on silence), adaptive resolution.

> **Interview angle.** "Why not just use FLOPs as the NAS objective?" — FLOPs correlate poorly with latency/energy across hardware
> (memory-bound layers, unsupported ops, parallelism); measure or model real latency on the target.

## D5 · Low-rank factorisation, weight sharing and the compression pipeline

**Low-rank:** replace $W\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ by $UV$ with rank $r$ (from truncated SVD, Eckart–Young); params/MACs drop from $mn$ to
$r(m+n)$ — worthwhile when $r<\frac{mn}{m+n}$. Choose $r$ per layer by retained singular-value energy or sensitivity, then fine-tune. For
convs: split a $k\times k$ conv into $k\times1$ and $1\times k$, or Tucker/CP decompositions. Activation-aware SVD (whitening by input
statistics, e.g. SVD-LLM, ASVD) works far better for LLMs than plain weight SVD.

**Weight sharing / clustering:** k-means cluster weights to $2^b$ centroids, store indices + codebook (**Deep Compression**: prune →
quantise via clustering → Huffman coding, 35–49× size reduction for AlexNet/VGG). Codebook quantisation is supported by some runtimes
(palettisation in Core ML). Hashing tricks, tied layers (ALBERT).

**A practical compression pipeline (typical order):**

1. Choose/design an efficient architecture for the target (biggest win).
2. Distil from a strong teacher.
3. Structured pruning (+ distillation to recover).
4. Quantisation (PTQ → QAT if needed), possibly with low-rank/sparsity.
5. Compile for the target (fusion, layout, memory planning) and **measure on device**; iterate on the accuracy–latency Pareto front.

> **Interview angle.** "You need to make a model 10× smaller with <1% accuracy loss" — explain the pipeline above with expected gains
> per step (int8: 4×; structured pruning: 1.5–2×; architecture/distillation: the rest) and how you'd validate each step.

## Questions

??* d1 [pruning] | Unstructured vs structured pruning — which actually speeds up inference and why?
Unstructured pruning zeros individual weights; the weight tensors keep their shape, so dense kernels on CPUs, GPUs and NPUs do the same work
(they multiply zeros). It saves storage with compression, and speeds up only with specialised sparse kernels at very high sparsity. Structured
pruning removes whole channels, heads, neurons or layers, producing a smaller dense model that's faster on any hardware, but it usually loses more
accuracy for the same parameter reduction and needs fine-tuning/distillation. N:M (2:4) semi-structured sparsity is in between and is accelerated by
supporting hardware.

?? d1 [pruning] | What is the lottery ticket hypothesis?
Randomly initialised dense networks contain sparse subnetworks ("winning tickets") that, when trained in isolation from the same initial weights
(or weights rewound to early in training), reach accuracy comparable to the full network. They're found by iterative magnitude pruning with rewinding.
It explains part of why over-parameterisation helps optimisation, but finding tickets is expensive, so it's not a practical compression method.

??* d2 [pruning, llm] | How does Wanda prune LLMs and why does it work well?
Wanda scores each weight by |W_ij|·||X_j||₂ — its magnitude times the L2 norm of the corresponding input feature over calibration data — and removes the
lowest-scoring weights within each output row (or in 2:4 groups), without updating the remaining weights. Because LLMs have outlier input features,
small weights connected to large activations can matter more than larger weights connected to tiny activations; including activation norms captures
this. It's almost as accurate as SparseGPT's Hessian-based updates at a fraction of the cost.

?? d2 [pruning] | What is 2:4 sparsity and what speed-up can you expect?
In every contiguous group of four weights (along the input dimension), at most two are non-zero. NVIDIA Ampere and later Tensor Cores (and some NPUs)
store only the non-zero values plus 2-bit indices and skip the zero multiplies, giving up to 2× math throughput and halved weight storage; end-to-end
speed-ups are typically 1.2–1.7× because not everything is matmul. Accuracy is recovered by fine-tuning with the mask, or by SparseGPT/Wanda in one shot
for LLMs.

??* d3 [distillation] | Write the knowledge distillation loss and explain the temperature and the T² factor.
L = α·CE(y, softmax(z_s)) + (1−α)·T²·KL(softmax(z_t/T) || softmax(z_s/T)). A temperature T > 1 softens both distributions, exposing the teacher's relative
probabilities on incorrect classes (dark knowledge) that carry similarity information. The gradient of the soft term w.r.t. the student logits scales as
1/T², so multiplying by T² keeps its contribution comparable to the hard-label term when changing T. Typical T = 2–5, tuned on validation.

?? d3 [distillation] | Why can a much larger teacher sometimes produce a worse student than a medium-sized teacher?
The capacity gap: a very large teacher's function and confidence patterns may be too complex for the small student to imitate, so it fits them poorly; a
medium teacher's outputs are easier to match. Remedies: teacher-assistant (intermediate-size) distillation chains, using higher temperatures, on-policy
distillation (training on the student's own samples), feature-level hints, or simply longer training.

??* d4 [nas, efficient-design] | What is hardware-aware NAS and what is Once-for-All?
Neural architecture search that optimises accuracy together with measured or predicted latency/energy/memory on the target device rather than FLOPs —
e.g. MnasNet's RL reward combining accuracy and phone latency, FBNet's differentiable search with latency lookup tables. Once-for-All trains a single
weight-sharing supernet that supports many sub-networks (different depths, widths, kernel sizes and resolutions) with progressive shrinking; for each new
device you search for the best sub-network under its latency budget and deploy it without retraining, amortising the training cost.

?? d4 [efficient-design] | Give four design guidelines for architectures that run fast on real hardware.
Keep input and output channel counts balanced to minimise memory access cost; avoid excessive group convolutions (they increase memory traffic per FLOP);
reduce network fragmentation (many small parallel branches hurt parallelism and add launch overhead); count element-wise operations, which are memory-bound;
use operators and shapes the target kernels support and accelerate (aligned channel counts, supported activations such as ReLU/h-swish over exotic ones);
measure latency on device rather than relying on FLOPs.

??* d5 [low-rank] | When does replacing a linear layer with a low-rank factorisation save compute, and how do you choose the rank?
A rank-r factorisation W ≈ UV costs r(m+n) parameters/MACs instead of mn, so it saves only if r < mn/(m+n) (e.g. r < 512 for a 1024×1024 layer, but real
gains need much smaller r). Choose r per layer from the singular-value spectrum (retain e.g. 90–95% of energy) or by sensitivity analysis on the task metric,
preferring activation-aware SVD (weighting by input statistics) for LLMs; then fine-tune or distil to recover accuracy. Layers with flat spectra compress
poorly.

?? d5 [compression] | In what order would you apply compression techniques, and why?
Architecture choice first (largest impact, sets what kernels you use), then distillation into the target-size model, then structured pruning with distillation
to recover, then quantisation (PTQ, then QAT if needed) last because it depends on the final weights and ranges, then compilation for the target with
on-device measurement. Quantising before pruning or fine-tuning would require redoing calibration; and each step should be validated on the real task
metric and device latency, iterating along the Pareto front.
