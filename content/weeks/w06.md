## D1 · Optimisers from SGD to Muon

**SGD with momentum:** $v\leftarrow\mu v + g$, $\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta v$. Momentum averages gradients
over ~$1/(1-\mu)$ steps, damping oscillation across steep directions and accelerating along shallow ones.
**Nesterov** evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead point.

**Adaptive methods** scale each coordinate by its gradient history. **Adam:**

```math
m_t=\beta_1m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_t,\quad v_t=\beta_2v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_t^2,\quad \theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\frac{\hat m_t}{\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon}
```

with bias correction $\hat m_t=m_t/(1-\beta_1^t)$, $\hat v_t=v_t/(1-\beta_2^t)$ (the EMAs start at 0). The update
magnitude is roughly $\eta$ per coordinate regardless of gradient scale. **AdamW** applies weight decay
directly: $\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\lambda\theta$. Adam's state is 2 floats per parameter — for a model in
bf16 with fp32 master weights and Adam that's ~16 bytes/param, a key number for memory estimates.

**Lion** (sign of an interpolated momentum) uses one state buffer and uniform-magnitude updates.

**Muon** (Jordan et al. 2024) treats each 2-D weight matrix as a matrix, not a bag of coordinates: it takes the
momentum $M$ and replaces it by its nearest semi-orthogonal matrix $UV^\top$ (from $M=U\Sigma V^\top$), so every
singular direction gets the same step size. The SVD is approximated with a few **Newton–Schulz** iterations, which
are just matmuls (cheap on GPUs):

```math
X_{k+1}=aX_k+b\,(X_kX_k^\top)X_k+c\,(X_kX_k^\top)^2X_k,\qquad X_0=M/\|M\|_F
```

Muon is used only for hidden 2-D matrices; embeddings, the output head, norms and biases stay on AdamW. Moonshot
scaled it ("Muon is Scalable", Moonlight) and trained **Kimi K2** with **MuonClip** — Muon plus **QK-clip**,
which rescales query/key projection weights whenever max attention logits grow too large, avoiding loss spikes
over 15.5T tokens. **DeepSeek-V4** also trains most modules with Muon (AdamW for embeddings, head, norms and some
mHC parameters). Reported benefit: ~1.5–2× better token efficiency than AdamW at scale.

> **Interview angle.** "Why does Adam need warmup?" — early on, $\hat v$ is estimated from very few samples, so
> $1/\sqrt{\hat v}$ can be huge and noisy; warmup keeps the first updates small until the statistics settle (RAdam
> fixes this analytically).

## D2 · Learning-rate schedules and batch size

The learning rate is the single most important hyper-parameter.

* **Warmup** (linear, ~1–5% of steps): stabilises adaptive optimisers and large-batch training, and gives
  normalisation/attention statistics time to settle.
* **Cosine decay** to ~10% of peak: the long-time default.
* **Linear decay to zero**: often slightly better for LLM pre-training.
* **WSD (warmup–stable–decay)**: hold the peak LR, then decay quickly at the end (10–20% of steps). You can branch
  a decay from any checkpoint of the stable phase, so one run gives models for many token budgets — popular with
  MiniCPM, DeepSeek and others for continued pre-training.
* **One-cycle** (up then down, with inverse momentum): fast convergence for CNNs.
* **ReduceLROnPlateau**: reactive, common in smaller audio/vision projects.

**LR range test:** increase the LR exponentially over a few hundred steps, plot loss, pick ~1/10 of the LR where
loss diverges.

**Batch size.** Larger batches reduce gradient noise; up to the **critical batch size** you can scale the LR with
batch (linear scaling rule for SGD; roughly square-root for Adam) and keep sample efficiency; beyond it you only
waste compute. Small batches regularise slightly (more noise → flatter minima). **Gradient accumulation** gives a
large effective batch on small hardware, but BatchNorm still sees only the micro-batch.

> **Interview angle.** "Training loss spikes then recovers" — LR too high, bad data batch, fp16 overflow,
> attention logit growth (QK-norm / QK-clip fix it), or Adam's $\epsilon$ too small. Mention gradient-norm
> monitoring and skipping/rewinding batches, which large-scale runs do.

## D3 · Normalisation layers

**BatchNorm** normalises each channel over the batch (and spatial) dimensions, then applies learned $\gamma,\beta$.
In training it uses batch statistics and updates running averages; in **eval** it uses the running averages.
Consequences: forgetting `model.eval()` is a classic bug; small batches give noisy statistics; it couples
samples in a batch; and at inference it is a per-channel affine map that can be **folded into the previous
conv/linear layer** — free on embedded targets:

```math
W'=\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}}W,\qquad b'=\frac{\gamma(b-\mu)}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}}+\beta
```

**LayerNorm** normalises each sample over its feature dimension — no batch dependence, identical in train and
eval, works for variable-length sequences: the transformer default. **RMSNorm** drops the mean subtraction and
the bias: $x/\sqrt{\mathrm{mean}(x^2)+\epsilon}\cdot\gamma$; cheaper and just as good, used by Llama, Qwen,
DeepSeek. **GroupNorm** normalises over groups of channels per sample — good for small-batch vision and audio.
**InstanceNorm** = GroupNorm with one channel per group (style transfer). For **streaming audio**, normalisation
over time must be causal (cumulative layer norm) or per-frame.

**Placement in transformers.** Post-norm (original Transformer: $\mathrm{LN}(x+F(x))$) trains poorly when deep
without warmup; **pre-norm** ($x+F(\mathrm{LN}(x))$) keeps a clean residual path and is the default. Recent extras:
**QK-norm** (RMSNorm on queries and keys, Qwen3, OLMo 2, Gemma) to bound attention logits; sandwich norm; and
gated attention outputs (Qwen3-Next/3.5) to remove attention sinks.

> **Interview angle.** "Why doesn't BatchNorm work well for transformers?" — variable sequence lengths and padding
> contaminate statistics, small per-device batches, train/inference mismatch for autoregressive decoding, and
> sample coupling. LayerNorm/RMSNorm avoid all of these.

## D4 · Regularisation and generalisation in deep nets

* **Dropout** zeroes activations with probability $p$ during training and scales the rest by $1/(1-p)$
  ("inverted dropout"), so eval needs no change. It approximates an ensemble of thinned networks. Modern LLM
  pre-training mostly uses **no dropout** (one pass over huge data rarely overfits).
* **Weight decay** (AdamW) — still standard, typically 0.1 for LLMs; not applied to norms and biases.
* **Data augmentation:** SpecAugment (time/frequency masking, time warping) for speech; noise and RIR
  convolution; speed perturbation; mixup/cutmix for classification.
* **Label smoothing:** target $(1-\alpha)$ on the true class, $\alpha/(K-1)$ elsewhere; reduces over-confidence,
  improves calibration, can hurt knowledge distillation from the smoothed model.
* **Stochastic depth / drop-path:** randomly skip residual blocks.
* **Early stopping** and **EMA of weights** (evaluate an exponential moving average of parameters — standard in
  diffusion models and many audio models).

**Flat vs sharp minima.** Minima where the loss changes slowly in all directions tend to generalise better;
large-batch training tends towards sharper minima. **SAM** (sharpness-aware minimisation) minimises the worst-case
loss in a small neighbourhood, at roughly 2× cost.

> **Interview angle.** "Train accuracy 99%, val 70%" → more/augmented data, stronger regularisation, smaller
> model, check for a train/val distribution mismatch first. "Train and val both 70%" → underfitting: bigger model,
> train longer, check LR and bugs (overfit a single batch!).

## D5 · Floating-point formats, mixed precision and numerical debugging

| Format | Exponent bits | Mantissa bits | Max | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FP32 | 8 | 23 | 3.4e38 | master weights, accumulation |
| FP16 | 5 | 10 | 65,504 | needs loss scaling |
| **BF16** | 8 | 7 | 3.4e38 | same range as FP32, no loss scaling |
| FP8 E4M3 | 4 | 3 | 448 | forward weights/activations |
| FP8 E5M2 | 5 | 2 | 57,344 | gradients |
| FP4 E2M1 | 2 | 1 | 6 | with per-block scales (MXFP4/NVFP4) |

**Mixed-precision training:** compute matmuls in BF16/FP16, keep an FP32 master copy of weights for the
optimiser update (small updates would vanish when added to BF16 weights), accumulate reductions in FP32.
With FP16, small gradients underflow, so multiply the loss by a scale factor and unscale before the step
(`GradScaler`, which also skips steps with inf/NaN and adjusts the scale dynamically).

**FP8 training at scale** was pioneered in the open by **DeepSeek-V3**: fine-grained scaling (1×128 tiles for
activations, 128×128 blocks for weights) to contain outliers, and periodic promotion of partial sums to FP32 to
fix Tensor Core accumulation precision. **DeepSeek-V4** goes further with FP4 quantisation-aware training of the
MoE expert weights. (Week 18 covers both.)

**Gradient clipping** by global norm (e.g. 1.0) is standard for transformers and RNNs.

**NaN/inf debugging playbook:** check data (NaN inputs, empty batches, zero-length audio); `log(0)`, `sqrt(0)` in
backward, division by a zero norm — add $\epsilon$; softmax/log-sum-exp without max subtraction; FP16 overflow
(>65,504) in attention logits or activations; LR too high; `torch.autograd.set_detect_anomaly(True)` to locate the
op; log per-layer gradient norms.

> **Interview angle.** "BF16 vs FP16?" — BF16 trades mantissa precision for FP32's exponent range: no overflow,
> no loss scaling, slightly noisier values. FP16 has more precision but a tiny range. Most training today is BF16;
> FP16 still matters on edge hardware that lacks BF16.

## Questions

??* d1 [optimisers] | Write the Adam update and explain bias correction.
m = β₁m + (1−β₁)g; v = β₂v + (1−β₂)g²; m̂ = m/(1−β₁ᵗ); v̂ = v/(1−β₂ᵗ); θ −= η m̂/(√v̂+ε). Because m and v start at
zero, early EMAs are biased toward zero (by factor 1−βᵗ); dividing removes that bias, which would otherwise make
the first steps too small for m and, since v is in the denominator, wrongly sized overall. With β₂ = 0.999 the
correction matters for the first few thousand steps.

??* d1 [optimisers, chinese-models] | What is Muon, and how did Kimi K2 and DeepSeek-V4 use it?
Muon applies momentum, then orthogonalises the update matrix for each 2-D weight — replacing M = UΣVᵀ by UVᵀ,
computed approximately with a few Newton–Schulz matmul iterations — so all singular directions move equally,
instead of being dominated by a few large ones. It's used for hidden matrices only; embeddings, output head and
norms use AdamW. Kimi K2 used MuonClip: Muon plus QK-clip, rescaling W_q and W_k when the max attention logit
exceeds a threshold, which removed loss spikes in 15.5T-token pre-training. DeepSeek-V4 uses Muon for most modules
for faster convergence and stability.

?? d1 [optimisers, memory] | How much memory does training a 7B model with AdamW in mixed precision need, excluding activations?
Per parameter: BF16 weights 2 B + BF16 gradients 2 B + FP32 master weights 4 B + Adam m and v 4 B + 4 B = 16 B.
7B × 16 B ≈ 112 GB, before activations, temporary buffers and fragmentation. That's why ZeRO/FSDP shard optimiser
state, gradients and weights across GPUs, and why 8-bit optimisers or LoRA are used on small hardware.

??* d2 [schedules] | Why warm up the learning rate, and what is a WSD schedule?
Warmup keeps early updates small while Adam's second-moment estimate (and normalisation and attention
statistics) are unreliable, and helps large-batch training avoid early divergence. WSD = warmup, then a long
stable phase at the peak LR, then a short decay to a small LR. Any checkpoint from the stable phase can be
decayed to produce a finished model, so one run serves many compute budgets and continued pre-training is easy;
quality matches cosine schedules.

?? d2 [batch-size] | If you double the batch size, what should you do with the learning rate?
For SGD, roughly double it (linear scaling rule) with warmup, up to the critical batch size; for Adam, scale more
conservatively (≈√2) and re-tune. Beyond the critical batch size, larger batches stop reducing the number of steps
needed, so you waste compute. Also check that BatchNorm statistics and regularisation still behave.

??* d3 [normalisation, deployment] | How do you fold BatchNorm into a preceding convolution for inference?
At inference BN is y = γ(x−μ)/√(σ²+ε) + β per output channel, with fixed running μ, σ². Since x = W*in + b is
linear, scale each output channel's filter by s = γ/√(σ²+ε) — W' = sW — and set b' = s(b−μ)+β. The fused conv
produces identical outputs with one op fewer and no extra memory traffic; all embedded toolchains do this.

?? d3 [normalisation] | LayerNorm vs RMSNorm vs BatchNorm — when would you use each?
BatchNorm: CNNs with reasonable batch sizes; foldable at inference; but batch-dependent and different in train and
eval. LayerNorm: per-sample normalisation over features; transformers, RNNs, variable-length sequences. RMSNorm:
LayerNorm without mean-centring and bias; slightly cheaper with equal quality — the default in modern LLMs.
GroupNorm: small batches in vision/audio. For streaming, use per-frame or causal (cumulative) variants.

?? d3 [transformers, normalisation] | Pre-norm vs post-norm transformers?
Post-norm, LN(x + F(x)), normalises the residual stream after each block; gradients to early layers pass through
many LNs, so deep post-norm models are unstable without careful warmup, though they can reach slightly better
quality when they do train. Pre-norm, x + F(LN(x)), leaves an identity path through the whole network, so it is
stable at depth and is the default. Its drawback is that the residual stream's norm grows with depth
("PreNorm dilution"), which motivates QK-norm, sandwich norms, and residual re-weighting such as Kimi's Attention
Residuals and DeepSeek's mHC.

??* d4 [regularisation] | How does dropout behave at train vs test time, and why is "inverted" dropout used?
In training, each unit is zeroed with probability p and the survivors are multiplied by 1/(1−p), so the expected
activation is unchanged; at test time dropout is a no-op. Inverted scaling puts the correction in training so
inference code needs no changes — important for deployment. Forgetting model.eval() leaves dropout on and makes
predictions random and worse.

?? d4 [regularisation] | What does label smoothing do and when can it hurt?
It replaces the one-hot target with (1−α) on the true class and α spread over the others, which penalises
over-confident logits, improves calibration and often accuracy. It can hurt when you need the model's relative
probabilities over wrong classes — e.g. as a teacher for knowledge distillation — because smoothing erases that
"dark knowledge", and it can hurt tasks where calibrated confidence must reach near 1.

??* d5 [numerics] | BF16 vs FP16 — what's the difference and why does FP16 need loss scaling?
Both are 16-bit. FP16 has 5 exponent / 10 mantissa bits (max 65,504, smallest normal ≈ 6e-5); BF16 has 8 / 7,
the same range as FP32 but less precision. Gradients are often smaller than FP16's range and underflow to zero, so
FP16 training multiplies the loss by a scale (e.g. 2¹⁶), unscales gradients before the step, and adapts the scale
when overflow occurs. BF16's range makes scaling unnecessary, which is why it dominates training; master weights
and reductions still stay in FP32.

??* d5 [debugging] | Your training loss becomes NaN after 2,000 steps. How do you debug it?
Reproduce from a checkpoint just before. Check the batch (NaN/inf inputs, empty or zero-length samples). Log
gradient norms per layer and the max attention logit/activation to find where it blows up. Enable anomaly
detection to locate the op. Common causes: LR too high or warmup too short; FP16 overflow; log/sqrt/division of
zero (add ε, clamp); unstable softmax; a loss term with a bad edge case. Fixes: lower LR, gradient clipping, BF16,
QK-norm, ε in norms, skip bad batches.
