## D1 · A framework for ML system design interviews

Use the same skeleton every time (write it in the corner of the whiteboard):

1. **Clarify the product and constraints (5 min).** Users and use cases; success metric (product + ML metric); **latency budget**;
   **compute/memory/power** on the target; privacy (can data leave the device?); connectivity; languages/markets; scale (devices,
   requests); safety/regulatory.
2. **Data (7 min).** Sources, labelling, synthetic data/augmentation (RIR, noise), splits by speaker/car/device, long-tail and negative
   data (hours of non-wake-word audio), privacy and consent.
3. **Model (10 min).** Baseline first (classical DSP, small CNN), then the proposed model; on-device vs cloud split; cascades; size and
   compute estimates with numbers.
4. **Training & optimisation (5 min).** Losses, distillation from a big teacher, quantisation/QAT, pruning; what you'd ablate.
5. **Evaluation (8 min).** Offline metrics on slices; on-device latency/memory/energy; online metrics/A-B; listening tests or human
   evaluation; acceptance thresholds.
6. **Deployment & monitoring (5 min).** Runtime/toolchain, streaming state, OTA, shadow mode, telemetry, drift, feedback loop.
7. **Trade-offs and extensions (5 min).** What you'd do with more time, failure modes, risks.

**Habits that score well:** state assumptions with numbers; draw the pipeline; give a baseline; quantify (MACs, bytes, ms, FA/hour);
discuss at least two alternatives with trade-offs; bring up failure modes and monitoring without being asked; check in with the
interviewer about where to go deeper.

> **Interview angle.** Most candidates jump to the model. Spending the first five minutes on requirements and budgets — then
> referring back to them — is the single biggest differentiator.

## D2 · Design: in-car voice assistant with an always-on wake word

**Requirements to pin down:** wake word ("Hey <brand>") for all seats or driver only; false accepts ≤ ~1 per 24 h of driving audio,
false rejects ≤ 5% across noise conditions (highway at 130 km/h, windows open, music, kids talking); end-to-end response latency;
offline capability; multiple languages; DSP budget (e.g. ≤ 5 MIPS, ≤ 200 KB) for the always-on part.

**Pipeline:** multi-mic array → **AEC** with infotainment/TTS reference (must handle loud music) → **beamforming/zone detection** (which
seat is speaking) → light noise suppression → **two-stage wake word**: stage 1 tiny int8 DS-CNN/CRNN on the DSP every 10–20 ms
(high recall); stage 2 a bigger verifier on the application processor/NPU on candidate segments (cuts false accepts) → on trigger,
streaming ASR (on-device RNN-T/Conformer for commands; cloud for open-domain) → NLU/LLM → TTS (which feeds back into AEC as reference).

**Data:** positive recordings across speakers, accents, seats, cars; TTS-generated positives; massive negatives (podcasts, radio,
in-car conversations) for false-accept rate; RIR simulation of cabins plus measured car IRs; noise at speeds; hard negatives
(phonetically similar phrases).

**Evaluation:** DET curves FRR vs FA/hour per condition and per seat; latency from end of wake word to listening state; in-car drive
tests; regression on previous false-accept triggers (radio ads).

**Deployment details:** streaming state, int8 on HiFi DSP, threshold per car model/market; user-specific enrolment optionally;
telemetry of trigger rates (privacy-preserving) for monitoring.

> **Interview angle.** Explain why a cascade beats one model: the always-on stage must be tiny and power-cheap; most audio is rejected
> there; the verifier only runs on rare candidates, so it can be 50× larger at negligible average cost.

## D3 · Design: real-time noise suppression for hearing aids or hands-free calls

**Constraints:** hearing aids — total latency ≤ ~5–10 ms (or own voice sounds echoey/comb-filtered), ~1 mW budget, tiny memory,
binaural link; car hands-free/telephony — ≤ ~20–40 ms algorithmic latency, ITU-T hands-free requirements (P.1100/P.1110), double-talk
with AEC, codecs.

**Approach:** classic baseline (Wiener/MMSE-STSA with noise tracking); DNN: frame-based causal model predicting ERB-band gains + a
short deep filter for harmonics (DeepFilterNet-style), or a small CRN with GRU; STFT with short windows (hearing aid: ~4–8 ms windows or
a low-delay filterbank); int8 or int16 quantised; ≤ ~1–20 M MACs per second for hearing aids, more for automotive.

**Data:** clean speech (multi-language), noise (car, babble, transient), RIRs, varied SNR (−5 to 20 dB), level variation; for hearing aids
own-voice and feedback conditions; train/test split by speaker and noise type.

**Losses & metrics:** SI-SDR + multi-resolution spectral loss; evaluation with PESQ/POLQA, STOI/ESTOI, DNSMOS, plus listening tests and,
for hearing aids, audiologist-relevant measures (speech-in-noise intelligibility); check behaviour on clean input, music and very low SNR;
speech distortion vs residual noise trade-off with a user-controllable strength.

**Deployment:** fixed-point front-end parity, WCET per frame, fallback to classic NS on overload, parameter updates OTA, per-user tuning
(fitting software).

> **Interview angle.** Lead with latency: it determines the window length, lookahead and architecture. Then quantify the compute budget
> and show the model fits with margin.

## D4 · Design: an on-device LLM assistant, and a driver-monitoring camera

**On-device LLM assistant (phone or car head unit):**

* *Requirements:* tasks (commands, Q&A over manuals/personal data, summarisation), offline vs online, privacy, latency (TTFT < 1 s, ≥ 10
  tokens/s), memory (e.g. ≤ 3 GB for the model), languages.
* *Model:* 1–4B dense model (distilled from a large teacher), possibly hybrid linear attention for long context; int4 weights (QAT if you
  train), int8 activations, bounded KV; LoRA adapters per feature; MTP/draft head for speculative decoding.
* *System:* router — on-device for simple/private requests, cloud for hard ones (by confidence or task type); on-device RAG over manuals
  and user data (small embedding model + vector index); function calling into vehicle APIs with strict schemas and confirmation for
  safety-relevant actions; guardrails.
* *Eval:* task success on realistic prompts, function-call accuracy, hallucination rate on manual Q&A, latency/energy on device, safety
  tests; A/B on deployed fleet.

**Driver monitoring system (DMS):**

* *Requirements:* detect drowsiness/distraction (eyes closed, gaze off-road, phone use) within ~1–2 s; regulatory requirements (e.g. EU
  General Safety Regulation, Euro NCAP protocols); works at night (IR camera + illumination), with glasses/sunglasses; strict false-alarm
  limits; functional safety level.
* *Pipeline:* IR camera → face detection & landmarks → head pose and gaze estimation, eye openness (PERCLOS) → temporal model for states
  → HMI alerts with escalation; all on the automotive SoC's NPU at e.g. 30 fps.
* *Data:* diverse drivers (ethnicities, ages, glasses, masks), lighting, cars; staged and naturalistic driving; privacy (on-device only).
* *Eval:* per-condition recall/false alarm, latency, fairness across demographic groups, robustness tests.

> **Interview angle.** For anything safety-related, mention fail-safe behaviour (what happens when the model is uncertain or the sensor
> is blocked), fairness across user groups, and how regulations shape requirements.

## D5 · Design: fleet-scale sensor ML and the data flywheel

**Predictive maintenance / anomaly detection on vehicle sensors:**

* *Edge:* compute features on the ECU/gateway (spectral features of vibration/acoustic sensors, statistics over windows), run a small
  anomaly model (autoencoder or GMM on features, isolation forest) to decide what to upload; bandwidth and cost limits.
* *Cloud:* fleet-level models, labels from repair records (delayed, noisy, sparse), survival analysis for time-to-failure.
* *Challenges:* rare failures (extreme imbalance), distribution differences across models/regions/firmware versions, concept drift after
  updates, privacy.

**The data flywheel for any edge ML product:** deploy → collect signals (with consent) → mine hard cases (false accepts, low-confidence
segments, user corrections) → label (human + teacher models) → retrain/distil → validate → OTA update → repeat. **Shadow mode**
evaluates new models silently on the fleet. **Federated learning** trains on-device when raw data can't be uploaded (secure aggregation,
differential privacy), with the cost of heterogeneity and communication.

**Platform aspects interviewers may probe:** experiment tracking, dataset versioning, reproducible training, model registry, device
farms for latency tests, release gates, monitoring dashboards and alerting.

> **Interview angle.** End every design with "how this gets better over time" — the flywheel and monitoring — it's what separates
> production experience from prototype experience.

## Questions

??* d1 [system-design] | What structure do you follow in an ML system design interview?
Clarify the product, success metrics and constraints (latency, compute, memory, power, privacy, connectivity, scale); then data (sources, labels,
augmentation, splits, negatives); model (baseline first, then the proposal, on-device/cloud split, cascades) with size/compute estimates; training and
optimisation (losses, distillation, quantisation); evaluation offline, on device and online; deployment and monitoring (runtime, OTA, shadow mode,
telemetry, drift); and finally trade-offs, risks and extensions — checking in with the interviewer about where to go deeper.

??* d2 [system-design, audio] | Design the wake-word detection for an in-car voice assistant. What are the key decisions?
Requirements: false accepts per hours of driving, false rejects across noise conditions and seats, latency, power/compute on the DSP, languages. Front
end: multi-mic AEC with the infotainment reference, beamforming/zone detection, light NS. Detection: a two-stage cascade — a tiny int8 streaming model on
the DSP (high recall), then a larger verifier on the application processor/NPU for candidates. Data: diverse positives (including TTS), huge negative
corpora, cabin RIRs and speed noise, hard negatives. Evaluation: DET curves per condition and seat, FA/hour on long negative audio, drive tests.
Deployment: streaming state, thresholds per car/market, privacy-preserving telemetry and OTA updates.

?? d2 [system-design, audio] | Why is acoustic echo cancellation critical before wake-word detection in a car?
The car's own loudspeakers play music, navigation prompts and the assistant's TTS at high level, and the microphones pick them up; without AEC, the
wake-word model would trigger on media content (false accepts) and miss the user speaking over music (false rejects, barge-in failure). AEC with the
playback reference removes the loudspeaker signal so only cabin speech remains; residual echo suppression and double-talk handling are needed too.

??* d3 [system-design, audio] | How would you design a DNN noise suppressor for a hearing aid?
Start from the latency budget (~5–10 ms total) and power (~1 mW): use short windows or a low-delay filterbank, strictly causal processing, and a tiny
model (e.g. ERB-band gain prediction with a small GRU plus a short deep filter), quantised to int8/int16, within a few M MACs/s. Train on diverse clean
speech, noise, RIRs and SNRs including own-voice; use SI-SDR + spectral losses; evaluate with intelligibility measures, PESQ/DNSMOS and listening tests,
checking clean-input transparency and very low SNR. Ensure WCET fits the frame, a fallback to classic NS, and fitting-software control of strength.

??* d4 [system-design, llm] | Design an on-device LLM assistant for a car head unit.
Clarify tasks (commands, manual Q&A, messaging), offline needs, latency and memory budget. Choose a 1–4B model distilled from a strong teacher, int4
weights (QAT if possible) with int8 activations and a bounded KV cache; per-feature LoRA adapters; speculative decoding with a small draft head. Add
on-device RAG over the owner's manual and user data, function calling into vehicle APIs with schema validation and confirmation for actions, and a router
that sends complex or knowledge-heavy queries to the cloud when connected. Evaluate task success, function-call accuracy, hallucination on manual
questions, latency/energy and safety; deploy via OTA with staged rollout and telemetry.

?? d4 [system-design, vision] | What are the main challenges in a driver-monitoring system?
Robustness across drivers (age, ethnicity, glasses, sunglasses, masks), lighting (night, direct sunlight — hence IR cameras and illumination) and
camera placement; low false-alarm rates so drivers don't disable it; low latency for detecting drowsiness/distraction; meeting regulatory and
NCAP test protocols; fairness across demographic groups; on-device processing for privacy; and safe behaviour when the camera is blocked or the
model is uncertain.

??* d5 [system-design, mlops] | What is a data flywheel and how does it work for an edge ML product?
A loop where the deployed product generates the data that improves the next model: collect consented signals and samples from devices (hard cases such
as false accepts, low-confidence inputs, user corrections), label them (humans and teacher models), retrain or distil, validate offline and in shadow
mode, deploy via staged OTA, monitor, and repeat. It turns deployment scale into model quality; privacy constraints may require on-device selection,
federated learning or aggregated analytics.

?? d5 [system-design] | How would you detect impending component failures from vehicle sensor data?
Extract features at the edge (windowed statistics, spectral features of vibration/acoustic signals) to limit bandwidth; run a lightweight anomaly
detector on-device to flag unusual windows for upload; in the cloud, combine fleet data with repair records to train supervised or survival models
(time to failure), handling extreme imbalance, delayed and noisy labels, and differences across vehicle models and firmware versions. Evaluate with
precision at fixed alert budgets and lead time before failure, and monitor drift after software updates.
