## D1 · Scaling laws and compute-optimal training

**Kaplan et al. (2020):** loss falls as a power law in parameters $N$, data $D$ and compute $C$ over many orders of
magnitude, $L(N)\approx(N_c/N)^{\alpha_N}$. **Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al. 2022)** corrected the data scaling: for a
fixed compute budget $C\approx6ND$, loss is minimised with $N$ and $D$ scaled **equally** — roughly **20 tokens per
parameter**. Gopher (280B on 300B tokens) was badly under-trained; Chinchilla (70B on 1.4T) beat it.

```math
L(N,D)=E+\frac{A}{N^{\alpha}}+\frac{B}{D^{\beta}},\qquad \alpha\approx\beta\approx0.3
```

**Inference changes the optimum.** Chinchilla-optimal minimises *training* compute. If a model will serve billions of
tokens, it's cheaper overall to train a smaller model on far more data: Llama 3 8B saw 15T tokens (~1,900 tokens/param);
Qwen and small on-device models go further. For edge deployment this is the key insight — you want the smallest model
that reaches the quality, trained as long as data allows, then distilled and quantised.

**Other scaling axes:**

* **Data quality and mix** often beat raw quantity (filtering, deduplication, synthetic data, code/math up-weighting).
* **Sparsity:** MoE gives better loss per FLOP; DeepSeek, Kimi and Qwen report scaling laws over expert count and
  granularity.
* **Test-time compute:** reasoning models (o1, DeepSeek-R1) improve with more generated "thinking" tokens; accuracy
  scales with log of inference compute, a new axis that trades latency for quality.
* **Precision:** low-precision training/inference has its own scaling behaviour (over-trained models are harder to
  quantise).

> **Interview angle.** "You have 1e22 FLOPs. What model size?" → $C=6ND$ and $D\approx20N$ give $N\approx\sqrt{C/120}
> \approx 9$B params on ~180B tokens for Chinchilla-optimal; then say you'd go smaller with more tokens if inference cost
> matters.

## D2 · Mixture-of-Experts fundamentals

Replace the FFN with $E$ expert FFNs and a **router** that sends each token to $k$ of them:

```math
y=\sum_{i\in\mathrm{TopK}(s)} g_i\,\mathrm{FFN}_i(x),\qquad s=\mathrm{softmax}(W_rx)\ \text{(or sigmoid)},\quad g=\text{normalised top-}k\text{ scores}
```

Parameters scale with $E$, compute with $k$: you get a bigger model at the same FLOPs per token.

* **GShard** (top-2), **Switch Transformer** (top-1, simpler, capacity factor), **Mixtral 8×7B** (8 experts, top-2:
  47B total, 13B active).
* **Capacity factor:** each expert processes at most $C\cdot\frac{kT}{E}$ tokens per batch; overflow tokens are
  **dropped** (pass through the residual). Needed for static shapes on TPUs/GPUs; dropless MoE (MegaBlocks) uses
  grouped/block-sparse GEMMs instead.
* **Load imbalance & collapse:** the router rich-get-richer dynamic sends most tokens to a few experts; others never
  train. The classic **auxiliary load-balancing loss** (Switch):

```math
\mathcal L_{aux}=\alpha\,E\sum_{i=1}^{E}f_i\,P_i,\quad f_i=\text{fraction of tokens routed to }i,\quad P_i=\text{mean router probability for }i
```

minimised when routing is uniform. **Router z-loss** stabilises router logits. Too large $\alpha$ hurts quality —
the model is forced to balance instead of specialise.

* **What do experts learn?** Mostly token/syntax-level specialisation (punctuation, code tokens, languages), less
  "topic experts" than people assume.

> **Interview angle.** "Why does MoE need a load-balancing mechanism?" — router collapse and hardware efficiency:
> with expert parallelism, the slowest (most loaded) expert sets the step time.

## D3 · The DeepSeek-style modern MoE and its descendants

**DeepSeekMoE (2024)** changed the design space:

* **Fine-grained experts:** split each expert into $m$ smaller ones and activate $m\times$ more (e.g. 256 small
  experts, 8 active). Many more combinations → better specialisation at the same compute.
* **Shared experts:** 1–2 experts that every token uses, capturing common knowledge so routed experts needn't
  duplicate it.
* **DeepSeek-V3 (671B total, 37B active):** 1 shared + 256 routed experts, top-8, **sigmoid** affinity scores normalised
  over the selected experts, and **auxiliary-loss-free load balancing**: add a per-expert bias $b_i$ to the scores **only
  for top-k selection** (not for the gating weights); after each step, decrease $b_i$ for overloaded experts and increase
  it for underloaded ones by a fixed speed $\gamma$. Balance without a gradient term that fights the language-modelling
  objective. A tiny sequence-wise aux loss is kept as a safety net. Plus **node-limited routing** (each token's experts
  span ≤ 4 nodes) to bound communication, and **no token dropping**.
* **First layers dense:** the first 1–3 layers use dense FFNs (routing early representations is unstable / unhelpful).
  **DeepSeek-V4** uses **hash routing** (fixed, token-id-based) in its first MoE layers.

**Higher sparsity is the trend:** Kimi K2 — 1T total, 32B active, 384 experts, top-8 + 1 shared. Qwen3.5-397B-A17B
(~4% active). DeepSeek-V4-Pro — 1.6T total, 49B active. Kimi K3 — 2.8T, **16 of 896 experts** + 2 shared, with a
"Stable LatentMoE" (experts operate in a reduced latent dimension). GLM-5 — 256 experts, 8 active.

**MoE + RL:** routing can differ between the inference engine that samples rollouts and the trainer that computes
gradients (numerical differences flip top-k choices) — a source of instability. Fixes include routing replay and
sequence-level importance ratios (Qwen's **GSPO**), and MiniMax's **CISPO** (week 17).

> **Interview angle.** Explain aux-loss-free balancing precisely: the bias affects *which* experts are chosen, not *how
> much* they contribute, so balancing doesn't distort the output or add interfering gradients.

## D4 · MoE systems: expert parallelism, all-to-all, and the edge question

**Expert parallelism (EP):** place different experts on different GPUs. Each MoE layer then does: route → **all-to-all
dispatch** (send each token to its experts' GPUs) → expert FFNs (grouped GEMM) → **all-to-all combine** → weighted sum.
All-to-all is bandwidth-hungry; it's the main MoE scaling cost. DeepSeek open-sourced **DeepEP** (dispatch/combine kernels
using NVLink + RDMA, FP8 dispatch) and designed V3 around the H800's limited NVLink bandwidth: node-limited routing,
overlapping communication with computation (DualPipe), and warp-specialised communication kernels. V4 goes further with
fine-grained communication–computation overlap ("wave" scheduling of experts).

**Inference properties:**

* All experts must be resident (or fetched) → **memory scales with total params**, compute with active params.
* Decode at small batch is memory-bound: each token reads its $k$ experts' weights; with a larger batch many experts
  get touched, so efficiency improves with batch size and EP across many GPUs (DeepSeek serves decode with very wide EP).
* Expert load imbalance at inference → redundant copies of hot experts (EPLB).

**MoE on the edge** — a real interview topic now:

* Pro: low compute per token (e.g. Qwen3.5-35B-A3B ≈ 3B active) → fast decode **if** weights fit in memory.
* Con: memory footprint = total params (35B × 4-bit ≈ 18 GB) — too big for phones, fine for laptops; flash/SSD
  **expert offloading** with caching of hot experts and prefetching using router predictions; quantising experts more
  aggressively than attention (experts are robust); batch size 1 means poor expert reuse.
* For MCUs/DSPs, MoE is rarely worth it; small dense models + distillation win. For audio enhancement, small MoE
  (like S-MoE) can make sense if routing is cheap and experts specialise by condition.

**Upcycling:** initialise an MoE from a dense checkpoint by copying the FFN into each expert (+ noise) and continuing
training — Qwen1.5-MoE, Mixtral-like recipes.

> **Interview angle.** "Would you deploy an MoE on a phone?" — compare total vs active params against RAM and flash
> bandwidth, mention offloading and expert quantisation, and conclude based on the device class.

## D5 · Back-of-envelope estimation for LLMs

Interviewers love estimates. Memorise these:

* **Weights:** bytes = params × bytes/param (FP32 4, BF16 2, FP8/INT8 1, INT4 0.5 + scales ≈ 0.55).
* **Training memory (mixed-precision Adam):** ~16 B/param + activations.
* **Forward FLOPs:** $2N_{active}$ per token; training $6N_{active}$ per token.
* **Decode speed (batch 1, memory-bound):** tokens/s ≈ memory bandwidth ÷ bytes read per token (active weights + KV
  cache). E.g. a 7B INT4 model (~4 GB) on a phone with ~60 GB/s effective bandwidth → ≤ ~15 tokens/s; on an M-series laptop
  at ~400 GB/s → ~100 tokens/s theoretical.
* **Prefill speed (compute-bound):** tokens/s ≈ achievable FLOP/s ÷ $2N$. A 45 TOPS INT8 NPU running a 3B model at ~30%
  utilisation → ~2,000 tokens/s prefill.
* **KV cache:** $2Ln_{kv}d_h\cdot$bytes per token.
* **Training cost:** $6ND$ ÷ (GPU FLOP/s × MFU). DeepSeek-V3: $6\times37\text{B}\times14.8\text{T}\approx3.3\times10^{24}$
  FLOPs; reported 2.788M H800 GPU-hours for the full training.

> **Interview angle.** Always state assumptions, show units, and give the bottleneck ("decode is bandwidth-bound, so
> quantising weights to 4-bit roughly doubles speed vs 8-bit").

## Questions

??* d1 [scaling] | What did Chinchilla change about scaling, and why do today's small models ignore it?
Chinchilla showed that for a fixed training-compute budget, parameters and training tokens should scale roughly equally
(~20 tokens per parameter); earlier large models were under-trained. But compute-optimal training ignores inference cost.
A model served at scale or on-device is cheaper overall if it's smaller and trained far longer than Chinchilla-optimal
(Llama 3 8B on 15T tokens), because inference cost scales with parameters for every token served. Loss keeps improving
with more data, just more slowly.

?? d1 [scaling] | What is test-time compute scaling?
Improving accuracy by spending more computation at inference — longer chains of thought, sampling many candidates with
voting or a verifier, search — rather than a bigger model. Reasoning models trained with RL (o1, DeepSeek-R1, Kimi K2
Thinking) show accuracy rising roughly with the log of generated tokens. It trades latency and cost for quality and is
relevant to edge only when latency budgets allow.

??* d2 [moe] | How does a Mixture-of-Experts layer work, and why is load balancing needed?
A router (linear layer + softmax/sigmoid) scores E expert FFNs per token; the top-k experts process the token, and their
outputs are combined with the normalised gate scores. Parameters scale with E while compute scales with k. Without
balancing, the router collapses onto a few experts (which then train faster and get chosen more), wasting capacity, and
with expert parallelism the most loaded expert dictates step time and memory. Solutions: an auxiliary loss E·Σ fᵢPᵢ,
capacity limits with token dropping, or bias-based aux-loss-free balancing.

?? d2 [moe] | What is the capacity factor in MoE and what happens to tokens that exceed it?
Each expert gets a fixed buffer of C·(k·T/E) tokens per batch, giving static shapes for efficient batched compute. If more
tokens are routed to an expert, the extras are dropped from that expert — they pass through the residual connection
unchanged (or go to their next choice). C > 1 reduces dropping at the cost of padding compute. Dropless MoE implementations
use grouped or block-sparse GEMMs to avoid capacity limits.

??* d3 [moe, chinese-models] | Explain DeepSeek-V3's auxiliary-loss-free load balancing.
Each expert i has a bias bᵢ added to its affinity score only when selecting the top-k experts; the gating weights used to
combine outputs come from the original scores. After each step, bᵢ is decreased by γ if expert i was overloaded and
increased by γ if underloaded. This steers routing toward balance without an extra loss term whose gradients would
compete with language modelling, so quality is better than with a strong aux loss. A very small sequence-wise balance
loss is kept to avoid extreme imbalance within a sequence.

??* d3 [moe, chinese-models] | Why do DeepSeek-style MoEs use fine-grained and shared experts?
Fine-grained: splitting experts into smaller ones and routing to more of them (e.g. 8 of 256) multiplies the number of
expert combinations, enabling sharper specialisation at the same compute. Shared experts are always active and learn
common knowledge (syntax, frequent patterns), so routed experts don't each have to store it, reducing redundancy. The
design (DeepSeekMoE) was adopted by Qwen, Kimi K2/K3, GLM and others.

?? d4 [moe, systems] | What communication does expert parallelism require, and how did DeepSeek reduce its cost?
Each MoE layer needs two all-to-all exchanges: dispatch tokens to the GPUs hosting their experts, and combine results
back. DeepSeek-V3 limited each token's experts to at most 4 nodes (node-limited routing) to bound inter-node traffic,
used custom kernels (DeepEP) that exploit NVLink within nodes and RDMA across nodes, dispatched in FP8, and overlapped
communication with computation of other micro-batches (DualPipe), so the all-to-all was largely hidden — important on
H800s with reduced NVLink bandwidth.

??* d4 [moe, edge] | Would you deploy a 35B-A3B MoE model on a smartphone? Reason it through.
Compute per token is like a 3B dense model, so decode speed could be good, but memory is set by total parameters: 35B at
4-bit ≈ 18–20 GB, above typical phone RAM (8–16 GB) and app memory limits. Options: expert offloading to flash with
caching/prefetch (limited by flash bandwidth and latency at batch 1), more aggressive expert quantisation (2–3 bit),
pruning rarely used experts, or a dense ~3–4B model distilled from the MoE. On a laptop with 32 GB+ unified memory, the
MoE is a good choice; on a phone, a distilled dense model is usually better.

??* d5 [estimation] | Estimate the decode speed of a 7B model at batch size 1 on a phone and on a laptop GPU.
Decode is memory-bandwidth bound: each token reads all weights once. At INT4 (~0.55 B/param incl. scales) the weights are
≈ 3.9 GB. Phone LPDDR5X ≈ 50–80 GB/s effective → ~13–20 tokens/s upper bound. Laptop GPU with ~400 GB/s → ~100 tokens/s.
Real numbers are lower (KV-cache reads, kernel efficiency, thermals). Halving bytes per weight roughly doubles speed;
speculative decoding or MoE increase tokens per weight read.

?? d5 [estimation] | Roughly how much did it cost to train DeepSeek-V3 and how do you sanity-check the number?
DeepSeek reported 2.788M H800 GPU-hours for the full training (pre-training, context extension and post-training), about
$5.6M at an assumed $2/GPU-hour — excluding research, ablations, data and staff. Check: 6 × 37B active params × 14.8T
tokens ≈ 3.3e24 FLOPs; an H800 delivers ~1e15 dense BF16 FLOP/s (≈2e15 FP8); at ~35–40% utilisation with FP8, ~2.7M GPU-hours
is plausible. The key enablers were MoE (37B of 671B active), FP8 training and communication/computation overlap.
