## D1 · Linear regression three ways

Model: $y = Xw + \varepsilon$, with $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$.

**1. Least squares.** Minimise $\|y-Xw\|^2$. Setting the gradient $-2X^\top(y-Xw)$ to zero gives the normal
equations:

```math
\hat w = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y
```

Geometrically, $X\hat w$ is the orthogonal projection of $y$ onto the column space of $X$.

**2. Maximum likelihood.** If $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2 I)$, the log-likelihood is
$-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|y-Xw\|^2 + \text{const}$, so MLE = least squares. Squared loss *is* the Gaussian noise
assumption; MAE corresponds to Laplace noise (robust to outliers).

**3. MAP.** A Gaussian prior $w\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2 I)$ adds $\tfrac{1}{2\tau^2}\|w\|^2$: that's **ridge**,
$\hat w = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$ with $\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2$. A Laplace prior gives **lasso**
(L1), which produces exact zeros.

**Numerics.** Never form $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ explicitly: it squares the condition number. Solve with QR
(`np.linalg.lstsq`), Cholesky of $X^\top X+\lambda I$, or SVD. Ridge also fixes the singular case
($d>n$ or collinear features), because $X^\top X+\lambda I$ is always invertible.

**Gradient descent** costs $O(nd)$ per step versus $O(nd^2 + d^3)$ for the closed form, which is why large
problems use (stochastic) GD. Its speed depends on the condition number $\kappa$ of $X^\top X$: the number of
iterations scales with $\kappa$, so **standardise features**.

> **Interview angle.** "Why squared loss?" → Gaussian MLE, convex, closed form. "When not?" → outliers (use
> Huber/MAE), heteroscedastic noise (weighted LS), classification (use cross-entropy).

## D2 · Logistic regression, softmax and cross-entropy

For binary labels, model $p(y=1\mid x) = \sigma(w^\top x)$ with $\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$. The Bernoulli
negative log-likelihood is **binary cross-entropy**:

```math
\mathcal L = -\sum_i \big[y_i\log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\big],\qquad \nabla_w\mathcal L = \sum_i (p_i-y_i)\,x_i
```

The gradient "prediction minus target, times input" is the same form as linear regression — a property of
canonical link functions in generalised linear models. The loss is convex, but there's no closed form; use
gradient methods or Newton/IRLS. On linearly separable data, $\|w\|\to\infty$ without regularisation.

**Multiclass:** softmax $p_k = e^{z_k}/\sum_j e^{z_j}$ with loss $-\log p_y$; gradient w.r.t. logits is
$p - \text{onehot}(y)$.

**Numerical stability — the log-sum-exp trick.** $e^{z}$ overflows for $z>88$ in float32. Subtract the max:

```math
\log\sum_j e^{z_j} = m + \log\sum_j e^{z_j-m},\qquad m=\max_j z_j
```

Frameworks fuse softmax and NLL (`F.cross_entropy` takes **logits**) for exactly this reason. Feeding
probabilities that already went through softmax into `cross_entropy` is a classic bug.

**Why not MSE for classification?** With a sigmoid output, MSE's gradient has a factor $\sigma'(z)$ that
vanishes when the model is confidently wrong, so learning stalls; cross-entropy cancels that factor. MSE on
probabilities is also non-convex in $w$.

> **Interview angle.** Be ready to derive $\partial\mathcal L/\partial z = p-y$ on the whiteboard in under two
> minutes. It's the most-asked derivation in ML interviews.

## D3 · Regularisation and optimisation basics

**L2 vs L1.** L2 shrinks all weights smoothly; L1 drives some exactly to zero (feature selection). Geometric
picture: the L1 ball has corners on the axes, and the loss contours usually touch the constraint set at a
corner. Elastic net mixes both and keeps groups of correlated features together.

**Weight decay ≠ L2 for adaptive optimisers.** For SGD they coincide. For Adam, an L2 term in the loss gets
divided by the adaptive denominator, so heavily-updated weights are barely regularised. **AdamW** decouples:
$w \leftarrow w - \eta(\hat m/(\sqrt{\hat v}+\epsilon) + \lambda w)$. (Week 6 goes deeper.)

**Other regularisers:** early stopping (equivalent to L2 for linear models with GD), data augmentation,
dropout, label smoothing, noise injection, smaller models, ensembling.

**Convexity.** A convex loss has no bad local minima; linear/logistic regression and SVMs are convex. Deep nets
aren't, yet SGD finds good solutions — over-parameterisation makes most local minima nearly as good, and
saddle points (not local minima) are the main obstacle in high dimension.

**GD variants.** Batch GD: exact gradient, expensive. SGD: one sample, noisy but cheap; the noise helps escape
sharp minima. Mini-batch: the practical compromise, sized for hardware. With step size $\eta$ on an $L$-smooth
function, GD converges if $\eta<2/L$.

**Feature scaling** matters for GD (conditioning), for distance-based methods (kNN, k-means, SVM-RBF) and for
regularisation (the penalty treats all weights equally, so units matter). Trees don't care.

> **Interview angle.** "Your logistic regression's coefficients are huge and flip sign between runs" →
> collinearity or separability; add L2, check the VIF/correlations, standardise.

## D4 · Classification metrics and imbalance

Confusion matrix: TP, FP, FN, TN.

* **Precision** $=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}$ (of what I flagged, how much was right), **recall/TPR**
  $=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$, **FPR** $=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}$, **F1** = harmonic mean of precision and recall.
* **ROC curve:** TPR vs FPR over all thresholds. **AUC** = probability that a random positive scores higher
  than a random negative (it's the Mann–Whitney U statistic). It's insensitive to class balance — which is
  exactly the problem: with 1 positive per 10,000, a tiny FPR still means many false alarms.
* **PR curve / average precision** focuses on the positive class and shows imbalance honestly. Its baseline is
  the positive rate, not 0.5.

**Choosing a threshold** is a product decision: maximise F1, hit a recall target (medical), or bound false
alarms. For wake-word detection the spec is usually *false accepts per hour* at a given *false reject rate*,
evaluated on hours of negative audio.

**Calibration.** A model is calibrated if, among samples it scores 0.8, 80% are positive. Check with a
reliability diagram and **expected calibration error** (ECE: bin by confidence, average |accuracy − confidence|
weighted by bin size). Modern deep nets are typically over-confident; **temperature scaling** (divide logits by
$T$ fitted on validation) fixes it without changing the ranking, so accuracy and AUC are unchanged.

**Handling imbalance:** report PR-AUC/recall at fixed precision; use class weights or focal loss
$-(1-p_t)^\gamma\log p_t$; resample (undersample negatives, oversample positives, SMOTE); and tune the threshold.
Note that reweighting/resampling shifts predicted probabilities, so recalibrate afterwards.

> **Interview angle.** "99% accuracy on a fraud dataset" — first question back: what's the base rate?

## D5 · Regression, ranking and speech/audio metrics; significance

**Regression:** MSE (penalises large errors, matches Gaussian noise), MAE (median-optimal, robust), Huber
(quadratic near zero, linear in the tails), $R^2 = 1 - SS_{res}/SS_{tot}$ (can be negative on test data),
MAPE (undefined at zero targets and asymmetric — avoid).

**Ranking / retrieval:** precision@k, recall@k, MAP, **NDCG**
$=\frac{1}{\text{IDCG}}\sum_i \frac{2^{rel_i}-1}{\log_2(i+1)}$, MRR.

**Speech and audio** (your domain — expect to be asked):

* ASR: **WER** = (S + D + I) / N via edit distance; can exceed 100%. CER for character-based languages.
* Enhancement: **SI-SDR** (scale-invariant SDR, in dB — the standard training/eval objective), **PESQ** and
  **POLQA** (ITU-T perceptual quality, P.862 / P.863), **STOI** (intelligibility), **DNSMOS** (a learned
  non-intrusive MOS predictor, needs no clean reference).
* Wake word / KWS: false-reject rate vs false accepts per hour, DET curves.
* Speaker verification: **EER** (where FAR = FRR), minDCF.

**Is the improvement real?** Report confidence intervals. The **bootstrap** resamples the test set with
replacement and recomputes the metric (resample by *utterance or speaker*, not frame). For two systems on the
same test set use **paired** tests (paired bootstrap, McNemar for classifiers, Wilcoxon signed-rank for
per-utterance scores). A 0.1 dB SI-SDR gain on 50 utterances is usually noise.

> **Interview angle.** When you quote results from your speech-enhancement work, give the metric, the test
> condition (SNR range, noise types, unseen speakers?) and the size of the test set.

## Questions

??* d1 [linear-models, probability] | Show that least squares is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise, and ridge is MAP.
With $y_i = w^\top x_i + \varepsilon_i$, $\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ i.i.d., the log-likelihood
is $-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i-w^\top x_i)^2 - \frac n2\log 2\pi\sigma^2$; maximising over $w$ is
minimising the sum of squares. Adding a Gaussian prior $w\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2I)$, the log-posterior adds
$-\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\|w\|^2$, so MAP = ridge with $\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2$. A Laplace prior gives L1 / lasso.

?? d1 [linear-models, numerics] | Why shouldn't you compute (XᵀX)⁻¹ directly, and what do you do instead?
Forming $X^\top X$ squares the condition number, so small singular values get lost in floating point, and
explicit inversion is slower and less stable than solving. Use QR or SVD (`lstsq`), or Cholesky on
$X^\top X+\lambda I$ for ridge. For very large problems, use iterative methods (conjugate gradient, SGD).

??* d2 [logistic, derivation] | Derive the gradient of softmax cross-entropy with respect to the logits.
$\mathcal L = -\log p_y = -z_y + \log\sum_j e^{z_j}$. Then $\partial\mathcal L/\partial z_k = -\mathbb 1[k=y] +
e^{z_k}/\sum_j e^{z_j} = p_k - \mathbb 1[k=y]$. So the gradient vector is $p - \text{onehot}(y)$: bounded,
never vanishing when the model is wrong, which is why cross-entropy trains well. For the weights,
$\nabla_W = (p-y)x^\top$.

??* d2 [numerics, pytorch] | What is the log-sum-exp trick, and why does F.cross_entropy take logits rather than probabilities?
$\log\sum e^{z_j} = m+\log\sum e^{z_j-m}$ with $m=\max z$, so the largest exponent is $e^0=1$ — no overflow,
and no underflow of the whole sum. `cross_entropy` fuses `log_softmax` and NLL so it can apply this trick and
compute $\log p$ directly; taking `log` of an already-rounded softmax loses precision (log 0 = −inf). Passing
softmax outputs into `cross_entropy` applies softmax twice and silently flattens the gradients.

?? d2 [logistic] | Why is MSE a poor loss for a sigmoid classifier?
The MSE gradient w.r.t. the logit is $2(p-y)\sigma'(z)$, and $\sigma'(z)\to0$ when $|z|$ is large, including
when the model is confidently wrong — learning stalls. Cross-entropy's gradient is $p-y$, with no such factor.
MSE through a sigmoid is also non-convex in the weights, and it doesn't correspond to the Bernoulli likelihood.

??* d3 [regularisation] | Why does L1 regularisation give sparse solutions and L2 doesn't?
Viewed as a constraint $\|w\|_1\le t$, the feasible set is a diamond with corners on the axes; loss contours
usually first touch it at a corner, where some coordinates are exactly zero. The L2 ball is round, so the touch
point is generically not on an axis. Equivalently, L1's subgradient has constant magnitude $\lambda$ near zero
(soft-thresholding sets small weights to exactly 0), while L2's gradient $2\lambda w$ shrinks as $w\to0$.

?? d3 [optimisation] | Is weight decay the same as L2 regularisation?
For plain SGD, yes: the gradient of $\frac\lambda2\|w\|^2$ is $\lambda w$, which is exactly the decay term.
For adaptive optimisers like Adam, no: an L2 gradient gets scaled by $1/\sqrt{\hat v}$, so weights with large
gradient history are barely regularised. AdamW applies decay directly to the weights, decoupled from the
adaptive step, which generalises better and makes $\lambda$ and the learning rate easier to tune.

??* d4 [metrics] | When is ROC-AUC misleading, and what do you report instead?
Under heavy class imbalance. ROC uses FPR = FP/(FP+TN); with a huge number of negatives, FPR stays tiny even when
false positives swamp the true positives, so AUC looks excellent while precision is poor. Report the PR curve /
average precision, precision at a required recall (or recall at a precision), or domain metrics like false
alarms per hour. ROC-AUC is fine for comparing rankers when classes are balanced or the costs are symmetric.

??* d4 [metrics, calibration] | What is calibration, how do you measure it, and how do you fix it?
A calibrated model's confidence matches its empirical accuracy. Measure with a reliability diagram and the
expected calibration error (bin by confidence; weighted average of |accuracy − confidence|), or with NLL/Brier
score. Fix post hoc with temperature scaling (divide logits by T fitted on validation NLL — keeps the ranking),
Platt scaling, or isotonic regression. Calibration matters when probabilities drive decisions: thresholds,
cascades, ensembling, or reporting risk.

?? d4 [metrics] | What does ROC-AUC mean probabilistically?
It is the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative (ties
count half). That's why it can be computed from ranks (it's the normalised Mann–Whitney U statistic) and why it
ignores the threshold and calibration — only the ordering matters.

?? d5 [metrics, speech] | How is WER computed and why can it exceed 100%?
WER = (S + D + I)/N, where substitutions, deletions and insertions come from the minimum edit-distance alignment
between hypothesis and reference, and N is the number of reference words. Insertions aren't bounded by N, so a
hypothesis with many extra words can exceed 100%. Normalise text (case, punctuation, numbers) consistently
before scoring.

??* d5 [metrics, statistics] | Your new model improves SI-SDR by 0.3 dB on the test set. How do you decide whether it's real?
Compare per-utterance scores of both systems on the same test set with a paired test: a paired bootstrap
(resample utterances — or speakers — with replacement, recompute the mean difference, look at the 95% CI) or a
Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Check the test set size, check the gain holds across slices (SNR, noise type,
unseen speakers), run multiple seeds to see training variance, and confirm with a perceptual metric
(PESQ/DNSMOS) or a listening test, since SI-SDR doesn't always track perceived quality.
