## D1 · RNNs, LSTMs and GRUs

A vanilla RNN: $h_t=\tanh(W_hh_{t-1}+W_xx_t+b)$. Backprop through time multiplies Jacobians
$\prod_t \mathrm{diag}(\tanh')W_h$; if the largest singular value of $W_h$ is below/above 1 the gradient
vanishes/explodes exponentially in sequence length.

**LSTM** adds a cell state with additive updates controlled by gates:

```math
\begin{aligned} i,f,o &= \sigma(W_{\{i,f,o\}}[h_{t-1},x_t]+b),\quad \tilde c=\tanh(W_c[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_c)\\ c_t&=f\odot c_{t-1}+i\odot\tilde c,\qquad h_t=o\odot\tanh(c_t)\end{aligned}
```

The cell update is additive, so when $f\approx1$ gradients flow through $c$ almost unchanged (the "constant error
carousel"). Initialise the forget-gate bias to ~1. In code the four gate matrices are fused into one
$4H\times(H+D)$ matmul — that's how `nn.LSTM` stores them (`weight_ih_l0` is $4H\times D$ in i, f, g, o order).

**GRU:** update gate $z$ and reset gate $r$, no separate cell; ~25% fewer parameters, often similar accuracy.

**Practicalities:** gradient clipping; truncated BPTT for long streams (carry the hidden state, cut the graph
every $k$ steps with `detach()`); packed sequences for variable lengths; bidirectional RNNs are non-causal.
RNNs are inherently sequential in time (hard to parallelise in training) but have **O(1) state per step at
inference** — great for streaming on a DSP. That trade-off is exactly what linear attention and state-space
models (week 13) try to get without the training bottleneck.

> **Interview angle.** "Why does the LSTM solve vanishing gradients?" — the cell path is additive with a
> learned, data-dependent forget gate, so the Jacobian $\partial c_t/\partial c_{t-1}=\mathrm{diag}(f_t)$ doesn't
> repeatedly multiply by a weight matrix.

## D2 · Seq2seq, attention's origin and decoding

**Encoder–decoder RNNs** (2014) squeezed the input into one vector — a bottleneck. **Bahdanau attention** let the
decoder look at all encoder states each step with learned weights: $\alpha_{ts}=\mathrm{softmax}_s(\mathrm{score}
(h_t,\bar h_s))$, context $c_t=\sum_s\alpha_{ts}\bar h_s$. Remove the recurrence entirely and you get the
Transformer (week 10).

**Training with teacher forcing:** feed the ground-truth previous token. This creates **exposure bias**: at test
time the model conditions on its own (possibly wrong) outputs. Mitigations: scheduled sampling, sequence-level
training, RL fine-tuning.

**Decoding strategies:**

* **Greedy:** arg-max each step — fast, can miss better sequences.
* **Beam search:** keep the top-B partial hypotheses by cumulative log-probability; expand each by the vocabulary,
  keep the top B. Needs **length normalisation** ($\log p/|y|^\alpha$) or it prefers short outputs. Standard for
  ASR and translation; less used for open-ended LLM generation, where it produces bland, repetitive text.
* **Sampling** with temperature, top-k, top-p (nucleus) for creative generation (week 12).

**Evaluation:** BLEU/chrF (translation), WER (ASR).

> **Interview angle.** Implementing beam search is a common coding question. Keep hypotheses as
> `(score, tokens, state)`, use `torch.topk` over the flattened `(beam × vocab)` scores, and handle finished
> hypotheses (EOS) separately.

## D3 · CTC and RNN-T: alignment-free sequence training

In ASR the audio (T frames) is much longer than the transcript (U tokens), and we don't know the alignment.

**CTC** adds a **blank** symbol and defines a many-to-one collapse $\mathcal B$: merge repeats, then remove blanks
(`--hh-e-ll-lll-oo` → `hello`; a blank is needed between the two l's). The loss marginalises over all alignments:

```math
p(y\mid x)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal B^{-1}(y)}\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_t(a_t\mid x)
```

computed with the **forward algorithm** over the extended label sequence $y'$ (blanks interleaved, length
$2U+1$) — a left-to-right HMM with a specific transition structure, exactly the forward pass you implemented for
HMMs. Work in log space. Assumptions: outputs are conditionally independent given the input (no language model
inside), and $T\ge U$ + repeats.

**Decoding:** greedy (arg-max per frame then collapse), prefix beam search, optionally with an external n-gram/LM
(shallow fusion).

**RNN-Transducer** adds a prediction network over previous labels and a joint network, removing the conditional
independence assumption; it streams naturally and is the standard for on-device ASR (Google, Apple, many
automotive assistants). Its loss is a 2-D (time × label) forward algorithm; memory is $O(T\cdot U\cdot V)$, which
motivated pruned RNN-T.

**Attention-based encoder–decoders** (Whisper, LAS) give strong offline accuracy but need the full utterance (or
chunked tricks) to stream.

> **Interview angle.** "Why does CTC need a blank?" — to emit nothing on frames with no new token, and to separate
> genuine repeated characters. Draw the trellis.

## D4 · Audio front-ends and modern speech models

**Front-end:** STFT (window 20–32 ms, hop 10 ms, Hann), magnitude or power, **mel filterbank** (perceptual
frequency warping), log compression (or PCEN for robust gain normalisation); MFCC = DCT of log-mel (decorrelated,
used with GMMs, less needed for neural nets). Normalise per utterance or with running statistics.

**Augmentation:** SpecAugment, additive noise at sampled SNRs, room impulse responses, speed/tempo perturbation,
codec simulation.

**Architectures:**

* **Conformer:** transformer blocks with a convolution module sandwiched by half-step FFNs (Macaron) — local + global
  modelling; the ASR encoder workhorse.
* **Self-supervised:** wav2vec 2.0 (contrastive over quantised targets), HuBERT (masked prediction of k-means
  cluster ids), WavLM (adds denoising; strong for speaker tasks).
* **Whisper:** large weakly-supervised encoder–decoder on 680K hours; robust, multilingual, but not natively
  streaming.
* **Neural audio codecs / tokenisers:** EnCodec, SoundStream, DAC, Mimi — conv encoder + **residual vector
  quantisation** (each stage quantises the residual of the previous) → discrete tokens at low bitrate, the input
  to speech LLMs and TTS (VALL-E, Moshi, Qwen-Omni-style models).
* **Speech LLMs:** an audio encoder + adapter feeding a text LLM, or fully token-based speech–text models;
  full-duplex conversation is an active area.

> **Interview angle.** Expect "how would you build wake-word + ASR for a car?": front-end and AEC, a tiny
> always-on KWS model on a DSP, then a larger streaming RNN-T/Conformer on the main SoC or in the cloud, with
> on-device fallback.

## D5 · Deep-learning speech enhancement and real-time constraints

You know this field; today's goal is to explain it crisply in interview language.

**Masking vs mapping.** Predict a time–frequency mask applied to the noisy STFT (IRM, phase-sensitive mask,
**complex ratio mask** cRM that also corrects phase) or directly predict the clean spectrum/waveform. Masks are
bounded and easier to learn; complex masks/mapping handle phase at low SNR.

**Architectures:** CRN/DCCRN (conv encoder + RNN bottleneck + decoder, complex variants), **DCUNet** (complex U-Net),
Conv-TasNet (learned basis, TCN separator), **DeepFilterNet** (ERB-band gains + deep filtering for periodic
components — efficient enough for embedded), dual-path RNNs, and generative approaches (diffusion/score-based like
SGMSE, flow matching) for high quality offline. Mixture-of-experts variants (like your S-MoE on DCUNet) specialise
experts by noise type or SNR.

**Losses:** SI-SDR (waveform), multi-resolution STFT magnitude loss, complex compressed spectral loss
($|S|^{0.3}$), perceptual metric losses (MetricGAN) — combine waveform + spectral.

**Real-time engineering:** causal (or small-lookahead) design; **frame-based processing** with state carried
between calls; latency budget (algorithmic + compute); real-time factor and worst-case (not average) compute; fixed
memory, no allocation in the audio callback; quantisation to int8 for DSP/NPU; avoid ops your target doesn't
support (GRU vs LSTM availability, complex ops).

**Evaluation:** PESQ, STOI, SI-SDR, DNSMOS, plus listening tests; watch for speech distortion vs noise
suppression trade-off, musical noise, and behaviour on clean input (should be near-transparent).

> **Interview angle.** Prepare a 2-minute story of your DCUNet/S-MoE work: problem, baseline, what you changed,
> metrics with test conditions, latency/size, and one thing that didn't work.

## Questions

??* d1 [rnn] | Write the LSTM equations and explain how they mitigate vanishing gradients.
Gates i, f, o = σ(W[h_{t−1}, x_t] + b); candidate g = tanh(W_g[h_{t−1}, x_t] + b_g); c_t = f⊙c_{t−1} + i⊙g;
h_t = o⊙tanh(c_t). The cell state is updated additively, and ∂c_t/∂c_{t−1} = diag(f_t), a learned, input-dependent
gate rather than a repeated weight matrix, so with f ≈ 1 gradients flow across many steps. Initialising the forget
bias to ~1 helps. The hidden state still passes through non-linearities, so very long dependencies remain hard.

?? d1 [rnn, streaming] | What's the trade-off between RNNs and Transformers for on-device streaming audio?
RNNs have O(1) memory and compute per time step and naturally stream, but train sequentially (slow on GPUs) and
struggle with very long context. Transformers parallelise training and model long context, but full attention costs
O(T) per step at inference with a growing KV cache (O(T²) overall) and needs chunking/limited context to stream.
Linear attention and state-space models (Mamba, Gated DeltaNet) aim for parallel training with RNN-like inference.

??* d2 [decoding] | Explain beam search and its common pitfalls.
Keep the B highest-scoring partial sequences by cumulative log-probability. At each step expand every hypothesis
with every token, then keep the global top B (topk over B×V scores). Move hypotheses that emit EOS to a finished
list. Pitfalls: bias toward short outputs (use length normalisation / a length penalty), lack of diversity (beams
differ by one token), cost O(B·V) per step, and for open-ended LLM generation it yields repetitive text; larger beams
can even reduce quality.

?? d2 [seq2seq] | What is exposure bias?
In teacher-forced training, the decoder always conditions on ground-truth previous tokens, but at inference it
conditions on its own predictions; early mistakes put it in states never seen in training and errors compound.
Mitigations: scheduled sampling, sequence-level objectives (minimum risk training), RL fine-tuning, or making the
model robust with noise on its inputs.

??* d3 [ctc, probability] | How does CTC compute p(y|x), and how is it related to HMMs?
Insert blanks around and between labels to get y′ of length 2U+1. Valid alignments are paths through a trellis over
time × y′ where each step stays on the same symbol, advances by one, or skips a blank between two different labels.
The forward recursion α_t(s) = (α_{t−1}(s) + α_{t−1}(s−1) [+ α_{t−1}(s−2) if allowed]) · p_t(y′_s) sums over all
alignments in O(T·U); p(y|x) = α_T(end) + α_T(end−1). This is the HMM forward algorithm for a left-to-right model
with tied emissions. Use log-space and log-sum-exp.

?? d3 [ctc, asr] | CTC vs RNN-T vs attention encoder–decoder for ASR?
CTC: simple and fast, alignment-free, streams easily, but assumes outputs are conditionally independent given audio,
so it relies on an external LM. RNN-T: adds a prediction network over previous labels, removing that assumption while
still streaming — the on-device standard — but training is memory-heavy. Attention encoder–decoder (LAS, Whisper):
highest offline accuracy, implicit LM, but needs the full input (non-streaming without chunking) and can hallucinate
on silence/noise. Hybrid CTC/attention training is common.

??* d4 [audio, front-end] | Walk through computing log-mel features. Which parameters matter?
Pre-emphasis (optional) → frame with a window (e.g. 25 ms Hann, 10 ms hop) → FFT (e.g. 512 points) → power
spectrum → multiply by a mel filterbank (e.g. 40–80 triangular filters) → log(·+ε) → normalise (per utterance or
running mean/variance). Parameters: sample rate, window/hop (time–frequency resolution and latency), FFT size, number
of mels and frequency range, log offset, and normalisation — mismatches between training and deployment front-ends
are a common source of silent accuracy loss.

?? d4 [audio, ssl] | How do wav2vec 2.0 and HuBERT learn without labels?
wav2vec 2.0 masks spans of latent frames from a CNN encoder and trains a transformer to identify the true quantised
latent among distractors (contrastive loss), with a codebook diversity loss. HuBERT masks frames and predicts discrete
targets obtained by offline k-means on MFCCs (first iteration) then on the model's own features (later iterations),
using cross-entropy on masked positions only. Both then fine-tune with CTC on a little labelled data.

?? d4 [audio, codecs] | What is residual vector quantisation and why do neural codecs use it?
A single VQ codebook with enough codes for high fidelity would be enormous. RVQ quantises the encoder output with a
first codebook, then quantises the residual error with a second, and so on for N stages; bitrate = N·log₂(codebook
size)·frame rate. It gives a coarse-to-fine hierarchy, allows variable bitrate by dropping later stages, and produces
discrete tokens that speech LLMs and TTS models can generate.

??* d5 [speech-enhancement] | Masking vs mapping for speech enhancement — trade-offs?
Masking predicts a bounded gain (real or complex ratio mask) applied to the noisy STFT: easy to learn, preserves
detail, conservative; but a real-valued magnitude mask keeps noisy phase, which limits quality at low SNR, and masks
can't add energy that the noise has destroyed. Mapping predicts the clean spectrum or waveform directly: can
reconstruct phase and missing content, but is harder to train and riskier (artefacts, hallucination). Complex ratio
masks and complex mapping sit in between; generative (diffusion) models push mapping further.

?? d5 [speech-enhancement, deployment] | What changes when you take a speech-enhancement model from a GPU paper to a hearing aid?
Latency budget (a few ms total), so short frames, causal processing, no lookahead; a fixed small compute and memory
budget (a few MIPS–tens of MOPS, KB–MB of RAM), so a much smaller model, int8/fixed-point, supported ops only;
worst-case timing with no allocation in the audio callback; power consumption; robustness to own-voice, feedback and
very low SNR; and evaluation with perceptual metrics and listening tests rather than SI-SDR alone.
