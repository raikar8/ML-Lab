## D1 · Supervised fine-tuning and data

**SFT / instruction tuning:** continue next-token training on (prompt, response) pairs formatted with a **chat template**
(role tokens, system prompt, tool calls). Key details:

* **Loss masking:** compute the loss only on response tokens (labels = −100 / `ignore_index` for the prompt), otherwise the
  model learns to generate user prompts.
* **Packing:** concatenate examples to fill the context, with **document/attention masks** and position resets so examples
  don't attend to each other.
* **Data quality beats quantity:** LIMA showed ~1K carefully curated examples can align a strong base model; diversity and
  correctness matter most. Synthetic data from stronger models (distillation) is now the norm; reasoning SFT uses long
  chain-of-thought traces (DeepSeek-R1's "cold start" data; R1-distilled small models are pure SFT on 800K R1 samples).
* **Catastrophic forgetting:** mix in general data, lower LR, fewer epochs (1–3), or PEFT.
* **Hyper-parameters:** LR ~1e-5–2e-5 for full fine-tuning of 7B-class models; warmup; small number of epochs.

**Rejection sampling fine-tuning (RFT / STaR):** sample many responses, keep the ones a verifier accepts, SFT on them — a
cheap step between SFT and RL.

> **Interview angle.** "Why mask the prompt tokens in SFT?" — the goal is p(response | prompt); training on prompts wastes
> capacity and teaches the model to imitate users. (Some pipelines keep a small prompt loss for robustness.)

## D2 · Parameter-efficient fine-tuning: LoRA, QLoRA, DoRA

**LoRA:** freeze $W_0\in\mathbb R^{d_{out}\times d_{in}}$ and learn a low-rank update:

```math
h = W_0x + \frac{\alpha}{r}\,BAx,\qquad A\in\mathbb R^{r\times d_{in}},\ B\in\mathbb R^{d_{out}\times r},\ r\ll d
```

Initialise $A$ randomly and $B=0$, so training starts exactly at the pretrained model. Trainable params per matrix:
$r(d_{in}+d_{out})$ vs $d_{in}d_{out}$ — <1% of the model; optimiser state shrinks accordingly. The $\alpha/r$ scaling keeps the
update magnitude roughly stable when changing $r$ (rsLoRA uses $\alpha/\sqrt r$). Apply to attention projections and usually also
MLP matrices for best results.

**Deployment:** **merge** $W=W_0+\frac\alpha rBA$ for zero inference overhead, or keep adapters separate to **hot-swap** many task
adapters on one base model (multi-LoRA serving: S-LoRA, Punica; on-device, Apple and others ship one base model with per-feature
adapters). Merging into a quantised base requires re-quantisation.

**QLoRA:** keep the frozen base in 4-bit **NF4** (a data type with quantiles of a normal distribution), dequantise on the fly in the
forward pass, train LoRA adapters in BF16; plus double quantisation of the scales and paged optimisers. Fine-tunes a 65B model on
one 48 GB GPU.

**Variants:** DoRA (decompose weight into magnitude and direction; LoRA on the direction), PiSSA (initialise A, B from the top
singular vectors of $W_0$), LoRA+ (different LRs for A and B), prefix/prompt tuning, (IA)³, adapters.

**Why low-rank works:** fine-tuning updates have low intrinsic dimension — the change needed to adapt a pretrained model lies in a
small subspace (Aghajanyan et al.). LoRA tends to "learn less and forget less" than full fine-tuning.

## D3 · RLHF with PPO

**Pipeline (InstructGPT):** SFT → train a **reward model** on human preference pairs → optimise the policy with RL against the
reward model, with a KL penalty to the SFT/reference model.

**Reward model:** Bradley–Terry: $p(y_w\succ y_l)=\sigma(r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l))$, trained with $-\log\sigma(r_w-r_l)$. Usually the
LLM with a scalar head.

**Objective:**

```math
\max_\pi\ \mathbb E_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r(x,y)\big]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{ref}(\cdot\mid x)\big)
```

**PPO** optimises it with a clipped surrogate per token, using importance ratio $\rho_t=\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{old}(a_t\mid s_t)$
and advantages $\hat A_t$ from a learned **value (critic) model** via GAE:

```math
\mathcal L^{CLIP}=-\mathbb E_t\big[\min(\rho_t\hat A_t,\ \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t)\big]
```

Four models in memory (policy, reference, reward, critic) — expensive and finicky (value-function training, reward
normalisation, many hyper-parameters).

**Failure modes:** **reward hacking** (exploiting reward-model flaws: verbosity, sycophancy, formatting tricks), mode collapse /
loss of diversity, KL too small (drift) or too large (no learning), length bias.

The policy-gradient foundation is REINFORCE: $\nabla J=\mathbb E[\nabla\log\pi(y\mid x)\,(R-b)]$ with a baseline $b$ to reduce
variance — PPO's critic and GRPO's group mean are both baselines.

> **Interview angle.** "Why the KL term?" — keeps the policy near the distribution where the reward model is accurate and
> preserves fluency/diversity; without it the policy finds adversarial outputs that score high but are bad.

## D4 · Direct preference optimisation and offline methods

**DPO** (Rafailov et al. 2023) shows the KL-regularised RLHF objective has a closed-form optimal policy
$\pi^*(y\mid x)\propto\pi_{ref}(y\mid x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)$. Inverting gives an implicit reward
$r=\beta\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{ref}}+\text{const}$; substituting into Bradley–Terry yields a classification loss on preference pairs —
**no reward model, no sampling**:

```math
\mathcal L_{DPO}=-\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\Big[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w\mid x)}-\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\Big]\Big)
```

Log-probs are **sums over response tokens**. Only policy + frozen reference needed.

**Variants:** IPO (bounded objective, avoids over-fitting deterministic preferences), KTO (unpaired good/bad labels), ORPO (no
reference model, odds-ratio term added to SFT), SimPO (length-normalised, reference-free, with a margin), iterative/online DPO
(generate new pairs from the current policy).

**DPO vs online RL:** DPO is simple and stable but offline — it learns from a fixed dataset, can increase the probability of
out-of-distribution responses, and tends to lower the likelihood of *both* chosen and rejected answers. Online RL explores and
generally wins for reasoning and agentic skills, where rewards can be verified. Many pipelines use SFT → DPO for style/safety → RL
for reasoning.

> **Interview angle.** Derive DPO in five lines from the optimal-policy formula, and say where it breaks (offline, distribution

## D5 · GRPO and RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR)

**GRPO** (DeepSeekMath, 2024; used for DeepSeek-R1): drop the critic. For each prompt sample a **group** of $G$ responses, score
them (rule-based verifier: correct answer, passing tests, format), and use the **group-normalised reward** as every token's
advantage:

```math
\hat A_{i}=\frac{r_i-\mathrm{mean}(r_{1..G})}{\mathrm{std}(r_{1..G})},\qquad \mathcal L=-\frac1G\sum_i\frac{1}{|o_i|}\sum_t\Big[\min(\rho_{i,t}\hat A_i,\mathrm{clip}(\rho_{i,t})\hat A_i)-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}_{i,t}\Big]
```

with the KL to the reference estimated per token by the unbiased "k3" estimator $\frac{\pi_{ref}}{\pi}-\log\frac{\pi_{ref}}{\pi}-1$.
Saves the critic's memory and instability; the group mean is the baseline. If all $G$ answers get the same reward, the advantage is
zero — no learning signal from that prompt.

**The 2025–26 refinements** (worth knowing by name — they show you follow the field):

* **Dr. GRPO:** removes the per-response length normalisation and std normalisation, which bias toward long wrong answers and
  toward easy/hard prompts.
* **DAPO** (ByteDance Seed + Tsinghua): **clip-higher** (asymmetric clip, larger upper bound to avoid entropy collapse),
  **dynamic sampling** (drop prompts whose group is all-correct or all-wrong), **token-level** loss averaging, overlong-response
  reward shaping; no KL term.
* **GSPO** (Qwen, used for Qwen3): **sequence-level** importance ratio (geometric mean of token ratios) and sequence-level
  clipping — more stable, especially for **MoE** where token-level ratios are noisy because routing differs between the rollout
  engine and the trainer.
* **CISPO** (MiniMax-M1): clip the **importance-sampling weight** itself (with stop-gradient) rather than dropping clipped tokens'
  gradients, so rare but important "reflection" tokens (e.g. *wait*, *however*) still contribute; MiniMax reported ~2× faster RL
  than DAPO and still uses it for M2.5.
* **On-policy distillation:** the student samples, a teacher scores every token (reverse KL per token) — dense supervision with
  on-policy data; DeepSeek-V4 trains domain specialists with SFT + GRPO and merges them into one model via on-policy distillation.

**Rewards:** rule/verifier-based (math answers, unit tests, JSON validity, compiler) are hard to hack; model-based
(LLM-as-judge, rubric rewards, generative reward models — Kimi K2 used self-critique rubrics for non-verifiable tasks) are
flexible but hackable. Outcome vs **process** rewards (scoring intermediate steps). Watch entropy, response length and reward
curves; length grows naturally as models learn to reason longer (R1's "aha moment").

**Infrastructure:** rollouts dominate cost; asynchronous RL (generation and training on different GPUs, slightly off-policy) —
GLM-5's asynchronous RL infrastructure, Kimi's partial rollouts for long trajectories, MiniMax's tool-latency-aware RL; and
numerical consistency between inference and training engines (DeepSeek-V4 built batch-invariant, deterministic kernels).

> **Interview angle.** "Why did GRPO matter?" — it made large-scale RL on LLMs much cheaper (no value model) and, with
> verifiable rewards, let DeepSeek-R1-Zero learn long reasoning from a base model with RL alone.

## Questions

??* d1 [sft] | What are the key implementation details of supervised fine-tuning for chat models?
Format examples with the model's chat template (role tokens, system prompt, tool-call syntax) exactly as used at inference; compute
the loss only on assistant tokens (mask prompts with ignore_index); pack sequences with attention masks that block cross-example
attention and reset positions; use a low LR (~1e-5) with warmup for 1–3 epochs; mix in general data to limit forgetting; and
prioritise data quality and diversity over volume.

??* d2 [peft] | Explain LoRA: the maths, initialisation, and how it's deployed.
LoRA freezes W₀ and adds a trainable low-rank update ΔW = (α/r)·BA with A ∈ R^{r×d_in}, B ∈ R^{d_out×r}, r ≪ d. A is random, B = 0, so
the model initially equals the pretrained one and training is stable. It trains <1% of parameters, shrinking optimiser memory and
checkpoints. At deployment you can merge W = W₀ + (α/r)BA (no latency cost) or keep adapters separate and swap per task/user on a shared
base model. It works because fine-tuning updates have low intrinsic rank.

?? d2 [peft] | What does QLoRA add to LoRA?
The frozen base model is stored in 4-bit NF4 (quantisation levels at quantiles of a normal distribution, suited to normally
distributed weights), with block-wise scales that are themselves quantised (double quantisation), and dequantised to BF16 on the fly
for the forward/backward pass; LoRA adapters are trained in BF16; paged optimisers spill optimiser state to CPU on memory spikes. This
lets you fine-tune very large models on a single GPU with little quality loss versus 16-bit LoRA.

??* d3 [rlhf] | Describe RLHF with PPO, including the models involved and the role of the KL penalty.
Start from an SFT model; train a reward model on human preference pairs with a Bradley–Terry loss; then optimise the policy with PPO to
maximise reward minus β·KL(π || π_ref). PPO needs the policy, a frozen reference (for KL), the reward model and a value/critic model for
GAE advantages; updates use a clipped importance-ratio objective so each step stays close to the sampling policy. The KL term keeps the
policy in the region where the reward model is reliable, preventing reward hacking and preserving fluency and diversity.

?? d3 [rlhf] | What is reward hacking? Give examples and mitigations.
The policy finds outputs that score highly under the (imperfect) reward but don't reflect the intended behaviour: excessive length or
hedging when the reward model likes long answers, sycophancy, exploiting formatting, test-case special-casing in code RL, or outputting
the answer format without valid reasoning. Mitigations: KL regularisation, reward-model ensembles and regular retraining on new policy
samples, verifiable rewards, length penalties/normalisation, rubric-based rewards, held-out evaluations and human review.

??* d4 [dpo] | Derive the DPO loss and explain its advantages and limitations.
The KL-constrained objective max E[r] − βKL(π||π_ref) has optimum π*(y|x) = π_ref(y|x)exp(r/β)/Z(x), so r = β log(π*/π_ref) + β log Z.
Substituting into Bradley–Terry, Z cancels in the difference: p(y_w ≻ y_l) = σ(β[log π(y_w)/π_ref(y_w) − log π(y_l)/π_ref(y_l)]). Minimise
its negative log-likelihood over preference pairs. Advantages: no reward model, no RL sampling, stable and cheap. Limitations: offline
(learns only from the fixed dataset, can't explore), can raise probability of unseen outputs, often lowers chosen-response likelihood too,
sensitive to β and length; online RL usually wins for reasoning.

??* d5 [rl, chinese-models] | How does GRPO differ from PPO, and why was it important for DeepSeek-R1?
GRPO removes the value model: for each prompt it samples a group of G responses, scores them, and uses each response's reward normalised
by the group mean and standard deviation as the advantage for all its tokens, with PPO-style ratio clipping and a KL penalty to a
reference policy. This halves model memory and avoids training an unstable critic for long reasoning sequences. Combined with rule-based
verifiable rewards (answer correctness, format), it let DeepSeek-R1-Zero learn long chain-of-thought reasoning from a base model with pure
RL, and made RL at scale affordable.

?? d5 [rl] | Name three refinements of GRPO from 2025 and what each fixes.
DAPO: clip-higher (raise the upper clip bound to prevent entropy collapse), dynamic sampling (skip prompts whose group rewards are all equal
since they give zero advantage), token-level loss averaging and overlong reward shaping. Dr. GRPO: removes length normalisation and std
normalisation that bias toward long incorrect responses and uneven prompt weighting. GSPO (Qwen): sequence-level importance ratios and
clipping for stability, important for MoE. CISPO (MiniMax): clips the importance weights instead of zeroing gradients of clipped tokens so
rare reflective tokens still learn.

?? d5 [rl] | Why do MoE models make RL training harder, and how did Qwen and others address it?
The rollout engine (e.g. vLLM with its own kernels and precision) and the trainer compute slightly different logits; in an MoE, these tiny
differences can flip top-k expert choices, so per-token probabilities and importance ratios differ substantially between engines and
fluctuate between policy versions, making token-level clipping noisy and training unstable. GSPO uses sequence-level ratios that are more
robust; others use routing replay (reuse rollout routing in training), batch-invariant/deterministic kernels (DeepSeek-V4), or CISPO-style
clipped weights.

??* d5 [rl, distillation] | What is on-policy distillation and why is it attractive?
The student generates its own samples; a stronger teacher provides per-token targets (e.g. its log-probabilities, minimising reverse KL)
on those samples. Unlike off-policy SFT on teacher outputs, the student learns on the states it actually visits (no exposure bias), and
unlike RL with scalar rewards it gets dense, per-token supervision, making it far more sample-efficient. DeepSeek-V4 uses it to merge
domain-specialist models into one, and it's a strong way to distil large reasoning models into small, deployable ones.
