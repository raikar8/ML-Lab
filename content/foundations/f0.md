## D1 · Linear algebra you actually use

**Vectors and the dot product.** A vector $x\in\mathbb R^d$ is a point or a direction. The dot product
$a^\top b=\sum_i a_ib_i=\|a\|\|b\|\cos\theta$ measures alignment. Cosine similarity, attention scores and
the logits of a linear layer are all dot products. Norms: $\|x\|_2=\sqrt{x^\top x}$, $\|x\|_1=\sum|x_i|$,
$\|x\|_\infty=\max|x_i|$.

**Matrices are linear maps; shapes tell you everything.** $A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ maps $\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$.
$(AB)_{ij}=\sum_k A_{ik}B_{kj}$ requires the inner dimensions to match: $(m\times k)(k\times n)\to(m\times n)$.
Matrix multiplication is associative but **not** commutative, and $(AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top$. There are four
equivalent views of $y = Ax$, and switching between them is half of ML maths:

1. $y_i$ is the dot product of row $i$ with $x$;
2. $y$ is a **weighted sum of the columns** of $A$ with weights $x_j$;
3. $AB$ is the sum of outer products $\sum_k a_{:k}b_{k:}^\top$ (this is why low-rank factorisations work);
4. $AB$ applies $B$ first and then $A$ (composition of transformations).

**Rank, column space, solving.** The column space is everything $Ax$ can reach, and its dimension is the rank.
$Ax=b$ has a solution iff $b$ is in the column space. If $A$ is square with full rank, it is invertible.
**In code, never compute $A^{-1}$ to solve.** Use `np.linalg.solve`, which does an LU factorisation (Gaussian
elimination with partial pivoting) and is faster and more stable. For least squares $\min\|Ax-b\|^2$, use
`lstsq` (QR/SVD). The normal equations $A^\top A x = A^\top b$ square the condition number.

**Projection.** The closest point to $b$ in the column space of $A$ is $\hat b = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top b$, and
the residual $b-\hat b$ is orthogonal to every column. Linear regression is exactly this projection.

**Eigendecomposition.** $Av=\lambda v$: $v$ is only scaled by $A$. A symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an
orthonormal eigenbasis, $A=Q\Lambda Q^\top$. Covariance matrices are symmetric positive semi-definite
($x^\top\Sigma x\ge 0$), so their eigenvalues are ≥ 0: those are the variances along the principal axes (PCA).
**Power iteration** ($v\leftarrow Av/\|Av\|$) finds the dominant eigenvector. It is the idea behind PageRank, and
behind the spectral-norm estimate used for spectral normalisation and Muon.

**SVD, the most useful decomposition.** Every matrix factors as $A=U\Sigma V^\top$, with orthonormal $U,V$ and
non-negative singular values $\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dots$. Truncating to the top $k$ terms gives the **best
rank-$k$ approximation** (Eckart–Young). That underlies PCA, LoRA intuition, low-rank compression of linear layers
(week 24) and latent KV compression (MLA, week 18).

**Condition number** $\kappa=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$: how much relative errors in the input can grow in the
output. With $\kappa\approx10^{8}$ you lose about 8 digits, which is all of float32's precision. This is why we
add $\lambda I$ (ridge), why normalisation layers help optimisation, and why float16 training needs care.

> **Interview angle.** "What's the shape of the gradient of $Wx$ w.r.t. $W$?" or "Why does PCA use SVD rather
> than the covariance's eigendecomposition?" (SVD of the centred data avoids forming $X^\top X$, which squares
> the condition number.) "What is the rank of an outer product?" (1.)

## D2 · Probability and statistics

**Random variables, expectation, variance.** $\mathbb E[X]=\sum_x x\,p(x)$ (or an integral). Expectation is
linear: $\mathbb E[aX+bY]=a\mathbb E[X]+b\mathbb E[Y]$, **always, even when X and Y are dependent**.
$\mathrm{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[X^2]-\mathbb E[X]^2$, and $\mathrm{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\mathrm{Var}(X)$. For independent
variables, variances add. That's why averaging $n$ independent estimates shrinks the variance by $1/n$ and the
standard error by $1/\sqrt n$.

**Conditional probability and Bayes.** $P(A\mid B)=P(A,B)/P(B)$, and

```math
P(\theta\mid x)=\frac{P(x\mid\theta)\,P(\theta)}{P(x)}\quad\text{(posterior = likelihood × prior / evidence)}
```

The classic interview trap is the **base rate**. Take a test with 99% sensitivity and a 1% false-positive rate,
for a condition with 0.1% prevalence: $P(\text{sick}\mid +)=\frac{0.99\cdot0.001}{0.99\cdot0.001+0.01\cdot0.999}\approx 9\%$.
The same arithmetic explains why a wake-word detector with a "tiny" false-accept rate still triggers several
times a day: there are millions of non-wake-word frames per day.

**Distributions to know cold.** Bernoulli/binomial (counts of successes), categorical/multinomial (classes,
tokens), Poisson (event counts per interval), Gaussian (sums of many small effects), uniform, exponential
(waiting times). The Gaussian log-density is
$-\tfrac12(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)-\tfrac12\log|2\pi\Sigma|$; squared-error loss is that density with
fixed variance.

**Maximum likelihood.** Choose parameters that maximise $\sum_i\log p(x_i\mid\theta)$. For a Gaussian:
$\hat\mu=\bar x$ and $\hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$. That variance estimate is **biased**; dividing
by $n-1$ (Bessel's correction) removes the bias, because $\bar x$ was fitted to the same data. MAP adds a log
prior, which is regularisation (week 2).

**The central limit theorem.** Sums and means of many independent variables with finite variance are
approximately Gaussian. That justifies confidence intervals like $\bar x\pm1.96\,s/\sqrt n$, and it is why
measured benchmark latencies need several runs.

**Estimators and uncertainty.** Bias = $\mathbb E[\hat\theta]-\theta$. Mean squared error = bias² + variance
(the same decomposition as the bias–variance trade-off). When no formula exists (for a median, an AUC, a WER
difference between two models), use the **bootstrap**: resample the dataset with replacement $B$ times,
recompute the statistic, and take percentiles.

**Hypothesis tests, briefly.** The p-value is the probability, *under the null*, of a result at least this
extreme. It is **not** the probability that the null is true. With many comparisons, expect false positives
(5% at α = 0.05); correct for that (Bonferroni) or pre-register one metric. For paired model comparisons (same
test utterances), use paired tests or a paired bootstrap.

> **Interview angle.** "You flip a coin 10 times and get 8 heads. Is it biased?" (Binomial tail
> $P(X\ge8)\approx 5.5\%$; inconclusive.) "What's the expected number of rolls to see a 6?" (Geometric: 6.)
> "Why divide by n − 1?"

## D3 · Calculus, gradients and optimisation

**Derivatives and the chain rule.** For $f(g(x))$, $f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$. Backpropagation is the chain rule applied
from the output back to the input, reusing intermediate results (reverse-mode autodiff, week 5).

**Gradients, Jacobians, shapes.** For a scalar loss $L(w)$ with $w\in\mathbb R^d$, the gradient
$\nabla_wL\in\mathbb R^d$ has **the same shape as w** and points in the direction of steepest increase. For a
vector function $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$, the Jacobian is $m\times n$. Results to memorise (from the Matrix
Cookbook):

| Expression | Gradient |
|---|---|
| $a^\top x$ | $a$ |
| $x^\top A x$ | $(A+A^\top)x$ ($=2Ax$ if symmetric) |
| $\|Ax-b\|^2$ | $2A^\top(Ax-b)$ |
| $\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$ | $\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$ |
| $\log\sum_j e^{z_j}$ | $\mathrm{softmax}(z)$ |
| $L=f(Wx)$, upstream $g=\partial L/\partial(Wx)$ | $\partial L/\partial W=g\,x^\top$, $\partial L/\partial x=W^\top g$ |

The last row is the whole of backprop through a linear layer. Check the shapes: $g$ is $m\times1$, $x^\top$ is
$1\times n$, so $gx^\top$ is $m\times n$, the shape of $W$.

**Check gradients numerically.** The central difference
$\frac{f(x+h e_i)-f(x-he_i)}{2h}$ has error $O(h^2)$, against $O(h)$ for a one-sided difference. With float64 and
$h\approx10^{-5}$, a correct analytic gradient matches to a **relative error** of about $10^{-7}$; anything above
$10^{-3}$ is a bug. Every from-scratch backprop you write should come with this check.

**Taylor expansion and why gradient descent works.** $f(x+\Delta)\approx f(x)+\nabla f^\top\Delta+\tfrac12\Delta^\top H\Delta$.
A small step against the gradient, $\Delta=-\eta\nabla f$, decreases $f$ by about $\eta\|\nabla f\|^2$. For a
quadratic with curvature $\lambda_{\max}$ (the largest Hessian eigenvalue), GD converges only if
$\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}$. Too large a learning rate diverges, and too small crawls along low-curvature
directions. That is the motivation for momentum, Adam and normalisation (week 6).

**Convexity.** A convex function has no bad local minima: every local minimum is global. Linear and logistic
regression (with or without L2) are convex. Neural networks are not, yet SGD finds good solutions in practice
because of over-parameterisation, symmetric minima and noise.

**Numerically safe exp/log.** $e^{1000}$ overflows. Use $\log\sum e^{z}=m+\log\sum e^{z-m}$ with $m=\max z$, and
compute $\log\sigma(z)$ as $-\mathrm{softplus}(-z)$. Interviewers check for this every time softmax appears.

> **Interview angle.** "Derive the gradient of logistic regression." "Why central differences?" "What happens
> if the learning rate exceeds 2/λmax?"

## D4 · Python for coding interviews

**Know the cost of what you call** (CPython; from the official time-complexity table):

| Operation | Cost | Trap |
|---|---|---|
| `list.append`, `pop()` (end), `a[i]` | O(1) amortised | — |
| `list.insert(0, x)`, `pop(0)`, `x in list` | O(n) | use `deque` / `set` |
| `dict`/`set` get, set, `in` | O(1) average | keys must be hashable (tuples, not lists) |
| slicing `a[i:j]` | O(j − i) **copy** | slicing in a loop makes the algorithm quadratic |
| `sorted`, `list.sort` | O(n log n), stable | `key=` beats `cmp`; sort by tuples for ties |
| string `+=` in a loop | O(n²) worst | build a list and `''.join` it |
| `heapq.heappush/pop` | O(log n) | it's a **min**-heap; negate for max |
| `bisect.bisect_left` | O(log n) | the insertion with `insort` is still O(n) |

**The standard library you should reach for:** `collections.Counter`, `defaultdict`, `deque`, `OrderedDict`;
`heapq`; `bisect`; `itertools` (`accumulate`, `combinations`, `permutations`, `product`, `groupby`, `islice`);
`functools.lru_cache` for memoisation; `math.inf`.

**Mutability and aliasing.** Assignment binds a name; it doesn't copy. `b = a` makes both names point to the same
list. `[[0] * C] * R` creates R references to **one** row, so write `[[0] * C for _ in range(R)]`. A mutable
default argument (`def f(x, acc=[])`) is created once and shared across calls; use `None` and create it inside.
Copying: `list(a)` or `a[:]` is shallow; `copy.deepcopy` copies nested structures.

**Iteration idioms.** `enumerate`, `zip`, `reversed`, comprehensions, and generators (`yield`) for lazy streams.
`sorted(items, key=lambda t: (-t.score, t.name))` sorts by score descending, then name. `divmod`, `//` (floor
division, which rounds toward −∞ for negative numbers), `%`.

**Recursion.** The default recursion limit is about 1000 frames, so a 10⁵-node linked list or degenerate tree will
crash a recursive solution. Use an explicit stack for DFS on large inputs, or say that you would.

**Writing code that interviewers like.** Small functions with clear names; guard clauses for edge cases; no
global state; type hints for signatures. Write a quick test as you go:
`assert two_sum([2, 7], 9) == (0, 1)`.

> **Interview angle.** "What's the complexity of `x in my_list` vs `x in my_set`?" "Why is `[[0]*3]*3` buggy?"
> "Explain the mutable-default-argument bug."

## D5 · NumPy, vectorisation and complexity

**Arrays, axes, dtypes.** An array is a typed block of memory plus a shape and strides. `axis=0` reduces over rows
(the result has one value per column). Mixing dtypes upcasts. Integer arrays silently wrap on overflow (int8:
127 + 1 = −128), which matters in quantization. `float32` has about 7 significant digits, `float16` about 3.

**Broadcasting rules.** Compare shapes from the right. Two dimensions are compatible if they are equal or one of
them is 1; missing leading dimensions count as 1. `(n, 1) - (1, m)` → `(n, m)`. That gives you pairwise
differences without loops, but it allocates the full `(n, m)` array, so watch memory.

```python
# pairwise squared distances, no Python loops, O(n·m·d) compute but O(n·m) memory
d2 = (X**2).sum(1)[:, None] + (Y**2).sum(1)[None, :] - 2 * X @ Y.T
d2 = np.maximum(d2, 0)        # round-off can make tiny negatives
```

**Views vs copies.** Basic slicing (`a[1:5]`, `a[:, 0]`) and `reshape`/`.T` usually return **views** that share
memory, so writing to a view changes the original. Fancy indexing (`a[[0, 2]]`, boolean masks) returns copies.
`np.shares_memory` tells you which. In PyTorch, `.view` needs contiguous memory and `.reshape` may copy.

**Vectorise.** A Python loop costs about 50–100 ns per iteration, while NumPy runs the loop in C, often 100× faster.
Typical rewrites: loops over samples → matrix products; `if` inside a loop → `np.where` or masks; running sums →
`np.cumsum`; one-hot → `np.eye(k)[labels]`; per-group sums → `np.bincount` / `np.add.at`.

**Big-O and estimating runtime.** Count the dominant operations as a function of input size, and state both time
and space. *Amortised* O(1) (like `list.append`) means occasional expensive resizes averaged over many cheap
operations. Rough budget for an interview answer: **about 10⁷ simple Python operations per second** (around 10⁸–10⁹
in C/NumPy). So n = 10⁵ allows O(n log n) but not O(n²) in Python; n = 20 allows O(2ⁿ); n ≤ 10 allows O(n!).

| n | Python can afford roughly |
|---|---|
| ≤ 10 | O(n!) — permutations |
| ≤ 20 | O(2ⁿ) — subsets / bitmask DP |
| ≤ 500 | O(n³) |
| ≤ 5 000 | O(n²) |
| ≤ 10⁶ | O(n log n) |
| ≥ 10⁷ | O(n), streaming, or vectorised |

> **Interview angle.** "Vectorise this loop." "Why is `a[mask] = 0` fine, but `b = a[mask]; b[:] = 0` doesn't
> change `a`?" "Given n = 10⁵, is your O(n²) solution acceptable?" (No: about 10¹⁰ operations.)

## Questions

??* d1 [linear-algebra, shapes] | What is the shape of ∂L/∂W for a linear layer y = Wx with W ∈ ℝ^{m×n}, and what is it?
The same shape as $W$, $m\times n$. With upstream gradient $g=\partial L/\partial y\in\mathbb R^m$ it is the outer
product $gx^\top$. For a batch $X\in\mathbb R^{B\times n}$ and $G\in\mathbb R^{B\times m}$ (row convention
$Y = XW^\top$), it is $G^\top X$: summing outer products over the batch. The input gradient is $W^\top g$.

?? d1 [linear-algebra, numerics] | Why should you solve Ax = b with a factorisation rather than computing A⁻¹?
Explicit inversion costs more (about 3× the flops of an LU solve), is less accurate, and the error grows with the
condition number. `solve` factorises once (LU with partial pivoting) and back-substitutes. For least squares, QR or
SVD avoid forming $A^\top A$, whose condition number is the square of $A$'s.

??* d1 [linear-algebra, svd] | What does the SVD give you, and name three places it appears in ML.
$A=U\Sigma V^\top$ with orthonormal $U,V$ and sorted singular values. Truncating to rank $k$ gives the best rank-$k$
approximation in Frobenius and spectral norm (Eckart–Young). It appears in PCA (the right singular vectors of the
centred data), low-rank compression of weight matrices and the LoRA intuition, latent recommenders
(matrix factorisation), whitening, and the spectral norm used for spectral normalisation and Muon's
orthogonalised updates.

?? d1 [linear-algebra] | Why are covariance matrices positive semi-definite, and why does it matter?
$v^\top\Sigma v=\mathrm{Var}(v^\top x)\ge0$ for every $v$. So the eigenvalues are non-negative and are the variances
along the principal axes; Cholesky works (with jitter if the matrix is singular); and Gaussian densities are well
defined. Numerically, an estimated covariance can come out slightly indefinite, so add $\epsilon I$.

??* d2 [probability, bayes] | A test is 99% sensitive with a 1% false-positive rate; prevalence is 0.1%. P(disease | positive)?
By Bayes: $\frac{0.99\times0.001}{0.99\times0.001+0.01\times0.999}\approx0.090$, about 9%. Most positives are false
because negatives outnumber positives 999:1. The same base-rate effect means a wake-word detector or fraud model
needs a very low false-accept rate, and you should report precision at a realistic prevalence, not just recall.

?? d2 [statistics, estimators] | Why does the sample variance divide by n − 1?
$\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$ is computed around the fitted mean $\bar x$, which is closer to the data than the true mean.
Its expectation is $(n-1)\sigma^2$, so dividing by $n-1$ makes the estimator unbiased (Bessel's correction). The MLE
divides by $n$: it is biased but has lower variance. The difference vanishes as $n$ grows.

??* d2 [statistics, uncertainty] | How would you put a confidence interval on the WER difference between two ASR models?
Use a **paired** bootstrap over test utterances (or speakers). Resample utterances with replacement, recompute
both systems' WER on the same resample, and record the difference; the 2.5% and 97.5% percentiles give the CI.
Pairing removes the variance shared by both systems. Resample speakers rather than utterances if utterances from
the same speaker are correlated. If the CI excludes 0, the improvement is significant at about 5%.

?? d2 [probability] | Expected number of fair-die rolls until the first 6? And the variance?
Geometric with $p=1/6$: $\mathbb E=1/p=6$, $\mathrm{Var}=(1-p)/p^2=30$. Derivation:
$E=1+\tfrac56E\Rightarrow E=6$.

?? d2 [statistics, p-values] | What does a p-value of 0.03 mean — and what doesn't it mean?
If the null hypothesis were true, a result at least this extreme would occur 3% of the time. It is **not** a 3%
probability that the null is true, it says nothing about effect size, and with many tests some will fall below
0.05 by chance. Report effect sizes with confidence intervals, and correct for multiple comparisons.

??* d3 [calculus, gradients] | Derive the gradient of the logistic-regression loss.
For one sample, $p=\sigma(w^\top x)$ and $L=-y\log p-(1-y)\log(1-p)$. Since $\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)$, $\partial L/\partial z=p-y$
with $z=w^\top x$, so $\nabla_wL=(p-y)x$. Over a batch: $\frac1nX^\top(p-y)$. The same "prediction minus target"
form appears for softmax with cross-entropy and for linear regression with squared loss: these are the canonical
links of the exponential family.

?? d3 [calculus, numerics] | How do you check a gradient numerically, and what result means "correct"?
Perturb each parameter by $\pm h$ and take $(f(x+h)-f(x-h))/2h$ (error $O(h^2)$). Compare with the relative error
$\frac{\|g_a-g_n\|}{\max(\|g_a\|,\|g_n\|)}$. In float64 with $h\approx10^{-5}$, expect about $10^{-7}$; above about
$10^{-3}$ is almost certainly a bug. Use float64, check a few random coordinates for big models, and turn off
dropout and other randomness.

??* d3 [optimisation] | Why does gradient descent diverge when the learning rate is too large?
Near a minimum the loss is approximately quadratic with Hessian $H$. Along an eigenvector with eigenvalue
$\lambda$, GD multiplies the error by $(1-\eta\lambda)$ each step; if $\eta>2/\lambda$, $|1-\eta\lambda|>1$ and
the error grows. The largest eigenvalue limits the step size, while the smallest sets how slowly the flat
directions converge. Their ratio, the condition number, is what preconditioning (Adam, normalisation) improves.

?? d3 [numerics] | How do you compute log(Σ exp(zᵢ)) without overflow?
Subtract the maximum: $\log\sum e^{z_i}=m+\log\sum e^{z_i-m}$ with $m=\max z$. All the exponents are then ≤ 0, so
nothing overflows, and the largest term is exactly 1, so the sum never underflows to 0. Softmax is
$e^{z-m}/\sum e^{z-m}$ by the same trick. For a sigmoid, use
`np.where(z>=0, 1/(1+e^{-z}), e^z/(1+e^z))`.

??* d4 [python, complexity] | What is the time complexity of `x in list`, `x in set`, `list.pop(0)` and `deque.popleft()`?
O(n), O(1) average, O(n) (it shifts every element), O(1). The usual quadratic-time bugs are a membership test on a
list inside a loop, `pop(0)` used as a queue, string concatenation in a loop, and slicing inside a loop.

?? d4 [python, gotchas] | Explain the mutable default argument bug and the `[[0]*3]*3` bug.
Default values are evaluated **once**, when the function is defined, so `def f(x, acc=[])` shares one list across
every call. Use `acc=None` and create the list inside. `[[0]*3]*3` repeats a reference to the **same** inner list,
so writing `grid[0][0] = 1` changes all three rows. Use a comprehension instead.

?? d4 [python, recursion] | Your recursive DFS passes the small tests and crashes on a 100 000-node path graph. Why, and how do you fix it?
CPython's recursion limit is about 1000 frames, and deep recursion also risks a C stack overflow. Rewrite it
iteratively with an explicit stack (and for post-order, push a "visited" marker or use two stacks). Raising
`sys.setrecursionlimit` is a band-aid that can still crash the interpreter.

??* d5 [numpy, broadcasting] | Compute all pairwise squared distances between X (n×d) and Y (m×d) without loops. Cost?
$\|x\|^2+\|y\|^2-2x^\top y$: `(X**2).sum(1)[:,None] + (Y**2).sum(1)[None,:] - 2*X@Y.T`. Compute is O(nmd), dominated
by one matrix product, and memory is O(nm). Clamp at 0 against round-off. The naive `((X[:,None]-Y[None])**2).sum(-1)`
allocates an n×m×d intermediate, which can be gigabytes.

?? d5 [numpy, memory] | When does NumPy return a view versus a copy, and why does it matter?
Basic slicing, `reshape` (when possible), `.T` and `ravel` (when contiguous) return views that share memory.
Fancy indexing, boolean masks and `flatten` return copies. Writes through a view change the original, while
writes to a copy don't. Views are free, but non-contiguous views can slow later operations, and PyTorch's `.view`
needs contiguity.

?? d5 [complexity] | n = 10⁵ and your Python solution is O(n²). Is that OK?
No. That's about $10^{10}$ basic operations; at about $10^7$ per second in Python it takes hours. You need
O(n log n) or better (sorting, a heap, hashing, two pointers). As a rule of thumb: n ≤ 5000 for O(n²), n ≤ 20 for
O(2ⁿ), n ≤ 10 for O(n!).

?? d5 [numpy, dtypes] | What happens when you add 1 to an int8 array holding 127, and why does it matter for quantized models?
It wraps to −128 silently (two's-complement overflow); NumPy doesn't raise for array arithmetic. That's why int8
matmuls accumulate in int32, why requantisation must saturate (clip) rather than wrap, and why you test integer
kernels on values at the edges of the range.
